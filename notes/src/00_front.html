The Big Picture: the whole book in 2 pages

Arnold's whole argument in one line: science in colonial India was never just "Western knowledge spreading to India". It was a social and political thing, used by the British for profit, control and pride, held back when it threatened British interests, shaped by Indian knowledge, labour and conditions, and later taken up by Indians to build their own modernity and nation.

Arnold's 6 big messages (use these in every essay)

  1. Science is not neutral. It is "a highly social activity" (p. 1). It carries money, power, politics and ideas about who is "superior". So we must always ask: who is doing science, for whom, and why?
  2. It was not a one-way gift. The old story says the West "diffused" (spread) science into a passive India. Arnold says no — it was an interaction / dialogue. Indians gave knowledge, skills, labour and materials; the British had to change their methods to fit India.
  3. But the interaction was unequal. It happened inside colonial rule. Britain and its scientists stayed on top; Indians were mostly kept in lower posts. Arnold calls this the "political character of science".
  4. For the British, science was mostly a tool and a symbol. A tool for revenue, trade, war and control. A symbol of being "civilised" and superior (the civilising mission).
  5. "Denial was often as important as diffusion" (p. 92). The British also stopped Indians from getting science and technology when it could harm British interests (shipbuilding, metals, locomotives, top science jobs).
  6. Indians were not passive. Some embraced science as the key to progress and nationhood (Mahendralal Sircar, P. C. Ray, J. C. Bose, C. V. Raman, Nehru). Some mixed it with Indian ideas. Some rejected industrial modernity altogether (Gandhi).

Company period
c. 1757–1858		 Science as "hobby + tool". Surveys, botany, geology, astronomy, mapping. Done mostly by army engineers and surgeons in their spare time. The Company cared only when there was profit, war or prestige in it — a "fitful flirtation".Ch 2
Steam age
c. 1820s–1900s		 Technology as power and proof of superiority: steamships, railways, telegraph, canals, roads, mints. Shaped by Indian conditions and labour. Benefits went mostly to Britain. Debates on whether India should industrialise.Ch 4

The "motive" question in one table

Why the British wanted science & technologyWhy the British held it back / didn't care
  • Money — revenue, trade, cash crops (indigo, cotton, tea), coal, timber.
  • War & security — maps, roads, railways, telegraph to move troops and get news fast.
  • Knowing India to rule India — surveys, census, gazetteers ("governmentality").
  • Prestige & legitimacy — "we are civilised, we bring progress"; very important after 1857.
  • Famine — botanic gardens, railways, canals, agricultural research were partly answers to famine.
  • Personal interest of officers, surgeons, governors-general (Bentinck, Dalhousie, Curzon).
  • Cost — the Company was "cash-conscious"; science was secondary to revenue, law and order.
  • Competition — didn't want India to compete with British copper, ships, locomotives, machinery.
  • Security — metal-working and arms could help Indian rebels (Arms Act 1878, mines closed).
  • Racism — Indians seen as "unfit" for original science.
  • Romantic idea — India should stay a land of peasants, princes and artisans.
  • Bureaucrats over scientists — the ICS kept scientists under control.
Why Indians wanted scienceWhy some Indians doubted it
  • To understand the source of Western power and progress (Sircar).
  • To reform society and religion by reason (Western-educated elite).
  • National pride: prove Indians can do "pure" science (Bose, Ray, Raman).
  • Self-reliance and industry (Swadeshi, Tata, Bengal Chemicals).
  • Modernity looked like copying the West — always "second-best".
  • Too materialistic (Keshab Chandra Sen).
  • Machines and railways bring poverty, disease, slavery (Gandhi, Hind Swaraj, 1909).
  • Intrusive colonial measures (plague control) caused resistance.

Words you must know (in simple English)

WordSimple meaning
Metropole / metropolitan / "centre"Britain (London) — the home of the empire: Royal Society, Kew Gardens, Court of Directors, British universities.
PeripheryThe colony — India. Seen as the "edge" of the scientific world.
Diffusion / diffusionistThe idea that science simply "spreads" from the West to the rest, like ink in water. Arnold criticises this.
Colonial scienceScience done in a colony. For Arnold: science (by Europeans and Indians) that is always tied up with colonial power.
Orientalism / OrientalistsEuropean scholars who studied Asian languages, texts, history (e.g. Sir William Jones). They admired ancient India but saw modern India as declined.
UtilitarianDone for practical use / profit, not just for knowledge.
"Improvement"18th–19th c. British idea: making land, crops and people more productive through knowledge.
Governmentality (Foucault)When the state collects detailed information on land, people and resources so it can manage and control them.
Panopticon (Foucault, used by Edney)A system where the ruler can "see everything". Used for the idea that maps/surveys let the British "see and know" all of India.
HegemonyDomination not only by force but through ideas that people come to accept.
Civilising missionBritish claim that they ruled India to "civilise" and improve it. Science and technology were their main proof.
TropicalityThe idea that tropical lands (like India) are different from Europe — rich nature but disease, famine and "weak" people who need European help.
Indigenous knowledgeLocal Indian knowledge — of plants, medicine, wells, ships, metals, forests, farming.
BhadralokThe educated Bengali middle class (mostly Brahmin, Kayastha, Vaidya castes).
ICS / IMSIndian Civil Service (top administrators) / Indian Medical Service (army & government doctors).
Swadeshi / Swaraj"Own-country made" (the 1905 movement to make Indian goods) / self-rule.
AnicutA dam/weir across a river (South Indian word), e.g. the Grand Anicut on the Cauvery.
Laissez-faireGovernment should not interfere in the market. (So in famine, the British built railways instead of controlling grain prices.)
