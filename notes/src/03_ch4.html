Chapter 4

Technologies of the Steam Age

Book pages 92–128 · Textiles, mining, ships & steamships, roads & railways, telegraph, irrigation, ideology, Swadeshi & Gandhi
In one line: Steam-age technology did not simply "transfer" from Britain to India. It was a "dialogue" shaped by colonial power (the British pushed what helped them and blocked what didn't), by Indian conditions, labour and older technologies, and it carried mixed meanings — progress for some, domination or ruin for others.

4.1 Arnold's opening argument

  • The usual story: the 19th century saw "a massive transfer of technology from the West to Africa and Asia", with colonialism as the channel — technology as a "tool of empire" (Daniel Headrick). India is the classic example.
  • Arnold: this is too one-dimensional. It praises Western dynamism but ignores the context. Technologies are rarely moved "wholesale". Transfer is more like a "dialogue" (Arnold Pacey) — especially in India, which already had many technologies and a very different environment.
  • Colonialism shaped the dialogue:
    • It cut India's old "inventive exchanges" with Asian and Indian Ocean neighbours and made Britain almost the only partner.
    • The state promoted technologies that served its military, economic or ideological needs and restricted Indian access to technologies that could hurt British interests.
"Denial was often as important as diffusion in shaping the technology of colonial India." — Arnold, p. 92
  • Old and new lived side by side — in farming, textiles, transport and labour. The relationship was often "synergistic" (helping each other), not just a fight.
  • Mixed messages: steam meant "progress" and "empire" to the British, but not necessarily the same to Indians. Both Europeans and Indians had doubts about whether modern technology suited India.

4.2 Textiles: decline, adaptation and survival

The old nationalist story: "de-industrialisation"

British mills first took India's export markets, then flooded India with cheap cloth. Weavers lost work and were pushed into farm labour → more poverty → famines. India's own mills (after 1850) could not absorb them.

What new research adds

  • Imported machines, dependence: over 90% of textile machinery for Indian cotton and jute mills (1850s–1930s) came from Britain (Lancashire) — worth Rs 780 million in 1905–39 alone. India barely developed its own machine-making before 1939.
  • But local practices shaped mills: the jobber recruited and managed workers (bridging managers and workers); Julahas (Muslim weavers from north India) worked in Bombay mills.
  • Pre-colonial India: probably the world's greatest cotton-textile producer until the late 1700s; the charkha (maybe from Iran, 14th c.); block printing, dyes, mordants; European "craze" for Indian calicoes.
  • But its strength was labour, not machines: cheap labour, plenty of cotton, and the skill and "hereditary knowledge" of spinners, weavers and dyers. Bengal looms were basic (no treadle); 20 stages from raw cotton to cloth, each by a caste specialist. Quality was "inversely related to technical sophistication" — the finer the cloth, the more labour. Weavers had no capital and depended on middlemen. So India was not about to have its own industrial revolution.
  • The Company supported silk filatures (to compete with Italy) but showed no interest in bringing cotton-spinning innovations to India. With no tariff protection, British cloth won: Indian cloth exports fell from Rs 165 lakhs (1816–17) to Rs 8 lakhs (1830–31); imports rose from Rs 3 lakhs to Rs 60 lakhs. Steamships, the Suez Canal (1869) and railways made it worse → new technological dependence on the West.
Handlooms adapted and survived
  • Village weavers disappeared; survivors moved to specialised urban centres (e.g. Salem, Sholapur) making ceremonial cloth, dhotis, saris.
  • Railways helped them sell wider; Indian migrants overseas created markets for handloom lungis.
  • Used cheap mill yarn; adopted the fly-shuttle and jacquard loom; after 1920, electric power.
  • In the 1930s, handlooms still made about one-third of India's textiles. "Dynamics of continuity" — not passive survival.

Changing image: at first Europeans wanted to learn from Indian textiles (Jones 1784, Hove 1787; Banks 1791 wanted to know "the minute practice of Indian workmen"). By the early 1800s (Buchanan) Indian tools looked "crude"; Indian crafts became the symbol of backwardness. By the late 1800s village crafts were nearly dead, sometimes seen as a cover for "criminal tribes and castes", and were put in the ethnographic museum as "relics of the past".

4.3 Mines and metallurgy

  • Past glory: Delhi's Iron Pillar; wootz steel admired in Europe into the 19th century.
  • Growing disdain: Valentine Ball (1881) said critics claimed Indian iron-workers "possess the art… [but] know nothing of the science" — though he admitted European foundries also lacked "scientific guidance". Fuel scarcity hurt too: 14 tons of charcoal per ton of iron. Not all decline was the fault of British rule.
  • Indian mining was seen as crude: no gunpowder, pit-props or pumps → short shafts that flooded or collapsed.
  • Examples: Himalayan copper mines — a Cornish miner (1838) suggested steam pumps, but mines were abandoned (1841) because English copper was cheaper even inside India. Kolar gold — rediscovered 1802, reworked only after 1871, deeper after electric power (1900). Rajasthan lead purer than British but one-eighth dearer. Panna diamonds — shallow pits, no investment.
  • Iron: Dharampal estimates 10,000 furnaces in 18th-century India, each ~20 tons a year — "wholly crushed out of existence" by British competition by 1880. India became a market for British iron (£15 million a year). The Porto Novo steel company (1833) made good steel but collapsed by the 1860s (charcoal shortage, transport costs).
  • Coal: Raniganj (dug from 1777) — bought by Dwarkanath Tagore in 1836; 40+ mines by 1880s; two-thirds of India's coal was home-produced. But very labour-intensive — Santal and Bauri families with picks and crowbars; small mines looked like "badly made tanks". Electricity improved mines only in the early 1900s.
Political denial — the key point

Metals and metal-working had supported Indian kingdoms' arms and cannon (from Akbar to the Marathas and Sikhs). So the British limited India's ability to mine and work metals, just as the Arms Act (1878) limited Indian access to firearms. Rajasthan's mines (Tod's "mines of Mewar") were abandoned; in 1857 the lead mines at Ajmer were closed because lead meant bullets.

4.4 Shipbuilding and steamships

  • India had an old shipbuilding tradition using teak. European trade first helped it: Indian-built ships were cheaper and tougher.
  • Hybrid ships = Indian carpentry + European design. Indian contributions: rabbeting (planks fitted so closely that no caulking was needed), coir ropes, lime to stop worms.
  • Bombay Dockyard: Parsi carpenters from Surat (1736); Lowji Nusserwanji Wadia became Master Builder — 35 ships in 40 years; his sons built 30 more. The Britannia (1778) impressed London so much that more ships were ordered; some joined the Royal Navy. 1736–1821: 159 ships (15 over 1,000 tons). Called "vastly superior to anything built anywhere else in the world".
  • Decline after 1820; last big Royal Navy ship 1831. The yard built teak hulls for imported engines (e.g. Hugh Lindsay, 1829, Bombay–Red Sea). Most steamships were assembled from British parts → British firms gained.
Why did Indian shipbuilding decline?

Headrick: technology — "what destroyed Indian shipbuilding was British iron".
Sangwan: politics — British shipbuilders lobbied Parliament to restrict Indian ships and cargoes, and feared steamships being made in India.
Arnold: the political point is valid, but the technological gap after industrialisation was also real.

Continuity: Parsis

Shipbuilding was a route for Parsis from crafts to modern science. Ardaseer Cursetjee — engineer, pioneer of gas lighting and photography in Bombay, Chief Engineer at the dockyard, the only Indian elected FRS in the 19th century. Later Parsis: J. N. Tata, Homi Bhabha.

  • Steam in the capitals: Boulton minted Company coins by steam (1786); the Calcutta Mint (1831) made 300,000+ coins a day — steam serving the colonial state.
  • Ganges steamers: Governor-General Bentinck came to India on the steamer Enterprise; iron steamers cut Calcutta–Allahabad from 3 months to 3 weeks — vital for mail and government.
  • Limits: rivers rise and fall with seasons, sandbanks, high costs. Worked best on the Brahmaputra (Assam tea, "coolie" labour). On the Indus (1837) a "technological trap": a strong engine made the boat too heavy (ran aground); a light one couldn't fight the current. "So the steamboats died a natural death."
  • Symbolic meaning: steamers were "conspicuous heralds" of Western science. In the novel Oakfield (1854) a character sees an "inconceivable separation" between the English "making a servant of the Ganges" and Indians "worshipping the same river". Arnold: but since many Indians quickly used steamers and trains, the gulf was not as wide as the British imagined.

4.5 Roads and railways

Roads first

  • Pre-steam transport was not frozen since antiquity — Mughals built roads; carts, bullocks, camels, and Banjara pack-bullock traders.
  • 1780–1840 the British built roads for military reasons: Calcutta–Benares road (1781); the Grand Trunk Road rebuilt from 1833 using pounded kankar ("Indian macadam"), reaching Peshawar after Punjab's conquest; Bhor Ghat road; roads in the south after Tipu's defeat. Like the Survey, roads imprinted colonial power on the land.
  • Lalla Tantimul, an Indian contractor, started a horse dak (mail and passenger) service in 1849. Letters Calcutta–Benares: 2.5 months by boat → 52 hours.
  • British loved comparing themselves to Rome — but roads lagged. Famine relief workers replaced convicts as road builders — a labour-intensive, low-tech method.
  • Narmada coal experiment (1838, 1848): Banjaras threw down the coal sacks "as they do grain" and said sharp coal would hurt their bullocks; boatmen lost boats in rapids; only half the coal reached Bombay after 8 months, costlier than Welsh coal. Lesson: inland coal needs railways.

Why railways? (the British motives)

  1. Coal — one of the first lines (1855) ran from Hooghly to the Raniganj coalfield.
  2. Trade — raw cotton from the Deccan; "intermesh" the economies of Britain and India: British goods in, Indian raw materials (cotton, oil-seeds, grain) out.
  3. Famine — the 1880 Famine Commission called for 20,000 more miles as "protective" (anti-famine) lines. Under laissez-faire, controlling grain prices was rejected, but building railways was seen as legitimate. So famine pushed technology towards agrarian needs, not an industrial base.
  4. Size and security — Dalhousie's minute (April 1853): "immeasurable" political benefits — quick news of "every event", fast troop movement, fewer soldiers dying on long marches. MacGeorge: railways shrank India to one-twentieth of its size. (Though three different gauges didn't help!)
  5. Ideology — Lord Hardinge (1848): steam would be "the greatest instrument of civilisation for the people, and of strength for the Government". Dalhousie: railways are "great engines of social improvement". MacGeorge: railways "awakened the Eastern world" and broke "deep-seated ancient prejudices" like caste.

The reality check

  • Huge scale: 40,000+ miles by 1947 (24,000 laid 1853–1900); 5th-largest system in the world by 1914; 600 million passengers a year by 1939.
  • But the 1928 Royal Commission on Agriculture still called railways "backward"; they relied on bullock-carts to bring cotton to railheads and grain to villages.
  • "A British show": Marx predicted railways would be "the forerunner of modern industry" in India — but most benefits went to Britain. Rails, sleepers, bridges, engines (even drivers) were imported. 14,000+ locomotives sold to India vs barely 700 made in India (1850s–1940s); British pressure blocked Indian production. Workshops (Lahore: 10,000 workers) mostly did repair and assembly — though some call them "schools of skills".
Not a straight "transfer" — Indian conditions and Indian methods
  • Environment: doubts in the 1840s about monsoons, floods, heat, animals; brushed aside by engineer Simms (1846) and Dalhousie — but easier said than done.
  • Rivers: dry sand in summer, floods in monsoon → very long bridges. European piles failed; engineers learned the Indian well-sinking technique (sinking weighted brick/masonry piers). The Son bridge: 7/8 mile, 28 spans, £330,000.
  • Bhor Ghat incline: 16 miles, 7 years, 42,000 workers at peak (1861).
  • Labour: artisan castes like the Wudders (tank- and well-diggers). Ian Kerr: engineers accepted Indian work practices because "the cheapness and relative abundance of Indian labour was worth many a machine". Customary tools (mattocks, crowbars, baskets) worked better than wheelbarrows or steam diggers. Indians learned new skills fast (riveting). Many women and children worked in family units.
  • Construction became "routine" only by the 1890s — 40 years of learning.

4.6 Telegraphs

  • The clearest case of military and political motives. Grew alongside (often ahead of) railways — trains need telegraph signalling.
  • Growth: 4,250 miles by 1856 → 17,500 (1865) → 52,900 (1900) → 100,000 miles and 17 million messages a year (1939). Unlike railways, telegraph stayed a state concern.
  • Uses: news of the fall of Rangoon (1852); early warnings in 1857 ("the telegraph saved India" — an exaggeration, but it helped). Soon used by businessmen and nationalist politicians too. Submarine cable to Britain (1870) tied India into the empire.
  • Personal interest of governors-general mattered: Bentinck (survey, steamers), Dalhousie the "technophile" (railways, telegraph, post), Curzon (imperial science).
  • W. B. O'Shaughnessy (Company surgeon, chemistry professor) built a 21-mile test line (1839) adapted for India — thicker cables covered with cloth and pitch for heat and humidity. Later first Director-General of Telegraphs. His chief assistant was an Indian, Shib Chandra Nandy, who built the first line (Calcutta–Diamond Harbour) and lines to Allahabad, Benares and Dacca — before such posts were "entirely Europeanised".

4.7 Irrigation

  • India had many water technologies: earth dams, step-wells, inundation canals, anicuts in Tinnevelly, and up to 40,000 wells per district in Salem and Coimbatore. Some were decaying by the early 1800s (war, British neglect of maintenance, revenue pressure).
  • But old works survived: in 1901, private works (tanks, wells) watered ~60% of irrigated land; state canals only ~40%.

South India: continuity with Indian methods

  • Grand Anicut on the Cauvery — "the greatest engineering work carried out in India before British rule".
  • Arthur Cotton (Royal Engineers) built a new anicut on the Coleroon (1835–38) to save the Tanjore delta — added 250,000 acres. Then the Godavari anicut (1847–52) after the 1833 famine.
  • Cotton found the Grand Anicut was made of earth and rubble encased in stone — and copied this cheap "rude mode of construction". This low cost helped get the project approved. He openly admitted his debt to the "old native engineers". (He also believed irrigation would help spread Christianity.)

North India: new engineering, shaped by local conditions

  • British first restored Mughal canals — Shah Jahan's Delhi canal (Capt. Blane, 1817–20), the Jumna Canal.
  • After the 1837–38 famine: the Ganges Canal (1842–54) under Proby Cautley (the fossil-hunter!). Head-works at Hardwar; the huge Solani Aqueduct; dug by "basket-labour". A steam locomotive brought in (1851) soon broke down and ended up driving machines at Roorkee engineering college. Irrigated 1 million acres; a model for the USA and Australia.
  • It had defects and was remodelled (1866–70) — "shaped bit by bit to the circumstances of the country". So it was neither a pure Western transfer nor an Indian copy — an "in situ" initiative.
  • Army engineers ran most canals, roads and survey works till 1900 — useful in peace, ready for war; they knew how to "deal with the natives". They saw themselves conquering nature like Romans ("battle with the river") — technocratic paternalism.
  • By 1901, about one-fifth of British India's cropped area was irrigated (one-third of that by the state).
The canal debate

Critics: waterlogging, reh (salt crust), malaria from leaky canals (Dempster 1845; Corbett 1870s said famines got worse). Elizabeth Whitcombe: canals were "a costly experiment".

Ian Stone: negative effects exaggerated; canals reduced drought, peasants used them "rationally"; wells and canals were complementary.

Official blind spot

The 1901–03 Irrigation Commission (set up by Curzon) barely looked at environmental costs. By the 1900s, focus shifted to scientific agriculture, and electric tube wells began to offer a more flexible way to irrigate.

4.8 Technology, ideology and resistance

Technology as the British "monument"

  • Railways and canals were monuments to British power and generosity — the Raj as a "technological empire" that could master nature, end famine and turn "workshy peasants into model farmers".
  • After 1857, direct interference in Indian society seemed dangerous, so technology became the safer way to "improve" India. Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1858) promised "works of public utility". The rebel Begum of Awadh replied that the Queen seemed to expect "no better employment for Hindostanees than making roads and digging canals".
  • W. W. Hunter (1862): railways would make British troops 20 times more efficient; commerce would be better security than "all the legions of England".
  • Deakin (1893): "If the British in India had achieved nothing else, the public works policy … would fully justify their supremacy". MacGeorge: no alien nation, "not excluding … ancient Rome", left more enduring monuments; better than "Mohammedan or Hindu creations" because they were useful.

"Indians hate technology"? — Arnold says be careful

  • Sir John Kaye claimed railways and telegraphs "disquieted" Hindus in 1857 and made Brahmins look "as feeble and impotent as babes and sucklings". Cutting telegraph wires in 1857 was used as "proof" of Indian hostility to technology.
  • Arnold's response:
    • Yes, rumours linked new technology to drought, disease and colonial coercion; railways did spread disease and ruin crafts. Medical measures (vaccination, plague controls) and survey parties (fear of new taxes) met real resistance.
    • But the British exaggerated resistance to project Indians as ignorant and themselves as rational.
    • Destruction in 1857 was strategic, not superstitious. Rebels used Enfield rifles themselves; Bahadur Shah's proclamation promised merchants free use of steam-vessels and steam-carriages.
    • Indians in Calcutta and Bombay welcomed the first railways; by 1858 the railway was "quite naturalised in the native mind". Trains carried pilgrims to melas; think of the train in Satyajit Ray's Pather Panchali.
    • Keshab Chandra Sen (1866) praised the "wonders" of steam and electricity — but warned modern civilisation was "materialistic": "ill fares the spirit".

4.9 Steam, Swadeshi and Swaraj: is industry right for India?

  • Western critics of industry (Ruskin, William Morris) admired Indian crafts.
  • George Birdwood (1878 Paris Exhibition catalogue): in India everything handmade is "a work of art"; craftsmen "polluted no rivers… nor poisoned any air"; mill workers were like the grinder of a barrel organ. He mocked a steam-plough that sank in Deccan soil and was then "worshipped as a lingam" — praising the "Mahratta plough", the "product of three thousand years' experience". (Farm machines generally failed; experimental farms became "museums" of rusting tools.)
  • E. B. Havell (1901): India should not copy Western industrialisation (slums, class conflict); instead revive the handloom with improved fly-shuttle looms. Adopted by the Basel Mission, provincial governments, princely states; P. C. Ghosh made looms; shown at the 1901 Industrial Exhibition alongside the Congress session (Gandhi attended).
  • Swadeshi (1905), after Curzon's Partition of Bengal: debate — revive crafts, or build Western-style industry? Many Indians rejected Birdwood/Havell's anti-industrialism as a plan to keep India "second-best" and dependent; they wanted to compete with Britain. Textile mills, match factories, steamship lines started — many failed (little capital, weak technology).
  • Gandhi, Hind Swaraj (1909): reject "modern civilisation". Railways "spread plague, encouraged famine" — "beyond dispute that they propagate evil". Machinery is "the chief symbol of modern civilisation" and "a great sin"; Manchester destroyed Indian crafts; Bombay mill-workers are "slaves". Answer: go back to villages, charkha and khadi as the tools and symbols of swaraj. Little impact in 1909, but huge impact after 1918–20.

4.10 Chapter 4 in a nutshell

Why the British introduced these technologiesWhy they held back / limited them
  • Military control and security (roads, railways, telegraph, survey)
  • Administration & communication over a huge land (mail, telegraph)
  • Trade & extraction (coal, cotton, export economy, British goods in)
  • Famine protection (railways, canals) — and revenue from irrigated land
  • Ideology: civilising mission, monuments, legitimacy after 1857
  • Protect British industry (ships, locomotives, machinery, copper, iron)
  • Security: metals = arms (Arms Act 1878; Ajmer mines closed 1857)
  • Cost & unsuitable conditions (Indus steamers, steam ploughs)
  • Romantic anti-industrial view of India (Birdwood, Havell)
The main point Arnold wants us to get
  1. Technology transfer was a "dialogue", not a one-way diffusion — and colonial power decided the terms: "denial was often as important as diffusion".
  2. The British chose technologies for military, economic and ideological ends. Most benefits went to Britain; India became technologically dependent.
  3. Indian environment, labour, skills and materials reshaped every technology (bridge piers, anicuts, cables, ships); old technologies survived beside new ones (handlooms, wells, carts).
  4. Technology carried strong ideological meaning — proof of British superiority — but Indians gave it their own meanings: use, enthusiasm, selective resistance, and finally critique (Swadeshi industry vs Gandhi's charkha).
