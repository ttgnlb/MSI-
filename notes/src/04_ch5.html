Chapter 5

Imperial Science and the Indian Scientific Community

Book pages 129–168 · Period: 1858 (Crown rule) to the 1920s, with the peak of "imperial science" under Lord Curzon (1899–1905)
In one line: After 1858, science and the colonial state became close partners — "imperial science" used for better government and to justify British rule — but it was controlled by bureaucrats, racially exclusive and mostly "applied". At the same time, Indians built their own scientific community and turned science (especially physics and chemistry) into a source of national pride. The clash of these two worlds is the story of this chapter.

5.1 The big shift after 1858

  • No sudden break from Company times, but a steady move from exploring and observing (with erratic Company interest) to a confident alliance between science and the state.
  • The state used science for two things: self-legitimation (justifying its rule) and more effective government.
  • But the state's view was instrumentalist: "science existed to serve the empire, not to constitute an alternative source of authority or to dictate imperial priorities".
  • From the 1890s to World War I, unprecedented Indian interest in Western science → an Indian scientific community that took part in international science.
  • These two things — imperial science + Indian scientific community — pushed science to new prominence in the 1890s–1900s, but also created tensions and contradictions.

5.2 Science under the bureaucrats (the ICS)

  • The Indian Civil Service (ICS) had little science training. It valued district experience, knowing villages and languages, and practical administration over "cosmopolitan" science. It kept the scientific services under its control. So state science was the science of the "enlightened bureaucrat" — politically dependent.
  • Census: first systematic all-India census in 1871, then every 10 years. More than numbers — a tool of "governmentality" that spread colonial ideas about India (religion, caste, health).
  • Gazetteers: W. W. Hunter (ICS) proposed them in 1869 — "nothing [is] more costly to a Government than ignorance". Imperial Gazetteer: 9 volumes (1881), 14 volumes (1885–7), revised 1907–9.
    • Written for "practical administrators", not for scientists; run by an ICS officer, not a scientist.
    • The 1909 volume moves step by step: geology, weather, plants, animals → people (ethnology, language, religion) → government departments (public health, police). People's lives were "sandwiched between the zoology of fishes and the origins of the Aryan religion".
    • H. H. Risley's essay on caste begins with skull measurement (craniometry) as a "test of race".
    • After 1857, the gazetteers worked like a "scientific title deed" — proof to India and the world that Britain knew India, based on printed texts rather than "native" testimony.
Scientists vs bureaucrats — constant tension
  • George Watt (Dictionary of the Economic Products of India, 1889): publicly said it was for administrators; privately raged at "Secretariat interference" — "the curse of every Indian branch of special or scientific work".
  • Ronald Ross resigned from the IMS (1899) in protest at poor research chances.
  • J. A. Voelcker (agriculture report, 1893): a department of agriculture run by an ICS generalist (trained in classics, maths, literature) who moves on after a short time makes no sense — but even he accepted some civilian control was needed.
  • Yet many British scientists accepted the system: India gave them unique opportunities and recognition.

Mixed results of state science

  • Fisheries: Francis Day, Inspector-General (1870), criticised the salt tax — no effect; the department was abolished when he retired.
  • Botanical Survey of India only in 1890 (George King) — mix of research and economic aims (cinchona, tea, coffee); small but effective; close links to Kew.
  • Meteorology: after the Calcutta cyclone (1864) and Orissa famine (1866) → Meteorological Department (1875) under H. F. Blanford; 100+ observatories by 1878.
  • Astronomy: N. R. Pogson (Madras) — 5,000 stars; his claim that sunspots affect droughts and famines (to the 1878 Famine Commission) got funding → Kodaikanal Observatory (1895), famous for solar physics.
  • Famine pushed science — but the first response was still railways, canals and the Famine Code (1880); science was only secondary.

5.3 "Imperial science" under Elgin and Curzon

  • A 1884 proposal for a department of science was rejected.
  • Lord Elgin (Dec 1898), during plague unrest, admitted the government was "almost without competent advisers in many branches of science" → "misdirected action". He asked Britain's Royal Society to guide research in India → Indian Advisory Committee of the Royal Society (inactive till 1903).
  • Arnold: this was a backward step — just when Indian scientists were emerging, the government showed no confidence in them and tied Indian science even more to London.
  • Curzon (1903) set up a Board of Scientific Advice (heads of the scientific services, meeting twice a year) — but it had to report every year to the Royal Society in London. Curzon's reason: the Government of India owned "the largest landed estate in the world", agriculture was the backbone, resources were poorly explored, and research money was limited.
  • Friction: London delays; only "applied", not "fundamental" research was thought suitable for India; Indian experts disliked London's "carping criticism". The link broke down by 1910; the London committee died after 1914, the Indian Board in 1922.
  • Roy MacLeod sees here a "new sense of scientific independence". Arnold disagrees: real scientific independence (like political) came only after 1945; the story actually shows the failure of scientists in India to win autonomy from the state and London.

What science meant to Curzon

  • Experts should advise, not administer. But science could be a force for change, a tool for efficient government, and — against rising nationalism — a "fresh source of legitimation for British rule".
  • 1899 speech to doctors: the British came to India as benefactors bringing law, religion, literature and science. People may question British law and religion, but not science — especially medicine: "built on the bed-rock of pure, irrefutable science", "a boon … offered to all"; it lifts purdah "without irreverence" and breaks caste "without sacrilege". Medicine alone was justification for British rule.
  • Pusa (April 1905): the central government must "give the lead" — model institutes, top (European) experts, results passed down to provinces and peasants. India as one "scientific empire", not separate provinces.
  • The catch: he asked princes and old elites to fund research, but was unenthusiastic about Indian-proposed projects. "The full credit for scientific and technological progress should rest with the imperial regime."
  • New institutes: veterinary (Muktesar, 1898), agriculture (Pusa, 1905), forestry (Dehra Dun, 1906). By 1914, nearly a dozen all-India ("imperial") services: medicine, meteorology, veterinary, botany, agriculture, forests, geology.
Table 5.1 (1920): who staffed the scientific services?

195 Europeans vs only 18 Indians across 11 services. Europeans were also paid far more — e.g. Agricultural Service Rs 1,000 vs Rs 460 a month; Educational Service Rs 1,910 vs Rs 490. Indians held less than 10% of scientific posts by 1920.

The "service ethos" — and its dark side: racism

  • The services were small, European and racially exclusive, with an "esprit de corps" — science as patriotic and paternalistic duty to the empire. The 1916–18 Industrial Commission (Sir Thomas Holland) even called scientific workers a "caste".
  • Deepak Kumar calls this "apartheid" in Indian science.
  • Pramatha Nath Bose (London-trained geologist) — the first Indian in the Geological Survey. Its head H. B. Medlicott (1880): "he is a Bengali and may be physically unfit"; spoke of his "scientific helplessness"; said Indians were "utterly incapable of any original work in natural science"; "not expect them to run before they can walk". Bose resigned (1903) when Holland, 10 years his junior, became Director. He joined the princely state of Mayurbhanj and found the iron ore that later fed Tata's steel works at Jamshedpur.
  • In 1874, Bengalis were kept out of Geological Survey apprentice posts for "deficiency of stamina and manliness"; "manly" Sikhs were chosen instead. Valentine Ball: Indians were "deficient in synthetical powers".
  • Universities were split into "two distinct racial camps". P. C. Ray (Edinburgh D.Sc.) was kept in the lower provincial service. J. C. Bose got into the Indian Educational Service but was paid one-third of British colleagues' pay until 1903.
  • Arnold's balance: racism did hold Indians back (especially in medicine), but it also made some, like Ray, more determined. Many Indians were also inspired by European teachers (Ray by Alexander Pedler; Bose by Father Lafont). Bose even got a special research grant (Rs 2,000 a year, 1897) — though an official sneered he might be "an original thinker (a rare thing in an Indian)" and that salaries are "paid for definite services", not for "high intellect" — showing dislike of research itself.

5.4 Medical science: from neglect to "imperial" research

1880s–90s: India was behind

  • Ross: no medical libraries; Pasteur's and Koch's discoveries "scarcely recognised, or … ridiculed".
  • Ernest Hart (editor, British Medical Journal, 1894): why were all tropical disease discoveries made by the French, Germans, even Japanese — not the British? Because research was crushed by administration; a man with a new scientific idea was seen as "a sort of pestilential fellow with a new bug"; promotion by seniority encouraged "respectable conservatism".
  • Change came from outsiders: E. H. Hankin (1892) and Waldemar Haffkine (1893; anti-cholera, then anti-plague vaccine in Bombay, 1896) — neither was IMS. Ross proved mosquitoes spread malaria (Nobel 1902) — "against the odds"; a colleague told him "We IMS men are not meant for research … we are simply doctors".

Plague (1896) — the turning point

  • Plague hit Bombay in 1896: 400,000+ deaths by 1900, ~8 million by 1914. Europe feared a new Black Death; at the Venice sanitary conference (1897) an embargo on Indian shipping was threatened.
  • To save trade, the government passed the Epidemic Diseases Act (1897): forced hospitalisation, destroying houses, examining rail travellers, banning fairs and pilgrimages → widespread resistance.
  • Malkowal (1902): 19 villagers died of tetanus from a contaminated anti-plague vaccine → Haffkine blamed and dismissed (1904); cleared years later.
  • 1898–99: government became pragmatic — eased coercion, accepted many deaths, and kept political control over medical policy. But the 1901 Plague Commission and British pressure made it take research seriously.

1900–1914: the research system

  • Research kept in the state, not universities. Central Research Institute, Kasauli (1906); Pasteur Institutes (anti-rabies, Kasauli 1900 and others); Bombay lab (later Haffkine Institute, 1925); King Institute, Guindy (1904); a Bacteriological Department (1906).
  • Indian Research Fund Association (IRFA), 1911 — funded research (Rs 5 lakhs a year; even before Britain's Medical Research Council); Indian Journal of Medical Research (1913); Calcutta School of Tropical Medicine (1921, Leonard Rogers).
  • Good: India became known for tropical-disease research (malaria, cholera, kala-azar, plague).
  • Bad: vaccine production swamped research (esp. in wars); institutes scattered; the IMS re-took control (Haffkine was called a "poor administrator"); research now became a reason to join the IMS (a Nobel like Ross!).
Hill-station science: racism + isolation

Research labs were put in cool hill stations — Kasauli (6,000 ft, near Simla), Muktesar (7,000 ft; moved from Poona partly because of public hostility to animal experiments). The belief: serious science needs a cool climate and "temperate" races. Viceroy Lansdowne (1891): original research needs qualities not found in "races bred in a tropical climate". A Bombay principal claimed a frog's heart beats 48 hours after removal in Scotland but only 5 minutes in Bombay! Result: medical research became a privileged, mostly white pursuit, denied to under-funded universities. Isolation also bred boredom, rivalries and Indian criticism that science was hidden from the public.

5.5 Forestry and agriculture

Forestry: science + revenue

  • Richard Grove: Company surgeons pioneered conservation — they linked deforestation to drying climate and famine; colonies were at the "cutting edge" of science.
  • Gadgil & Guha: the real motive was money — Forest Department (1864), Forest Acts (1865, 1878) to tap forest wealth and get timber for railways. Deepak Kumar: foresters were "basically estate-managers"; revenue "was the only obsession".
  • Arnold: both overstate. Forestry was profitable (a model for other departments) and genuinely scientific — it shows the interdependence of science and state. Huge domain (249,000 sq miles by 1916).
  • Dietrich Brandis (German botanist), Inspector-General of Forests from 1864 for 19 years — brought German/French scientific forestry; founded the forestry school at Dehra Dun (1878) to train Indian assistants; wrote forest floras; used Indian plant names. For him, science and revenue were "largely, and legitimately, indistinguishable" — but forests should ultimately benefit the people.
  • Conflict: restrictions on grazing, shifting cultivation, fuel → tribal revolts (1870s–80s) and forest satyagrahas (1920s–30s). Two knowledge systems clashed: professional forestry vs local customary knowledge.
  • But also engagement: tribals worked as labour; their knowledge helped identify species and collect drugs (e.g. kino by Kurumbars in Malabar). Foresters slowly learned that "primitive" practices — shifting cultivation, grazing, burning grass — were needed to regenerate sal and teak.

Agriculture: neglected, then pushed by famine

  • Before the 1890s, agriculture was neglected as a science — the state relied on big public works (canals, railways). Forestry got a service (1864) and school (1878) 30+ years before agriculture. New crops focused on commercial ones (tea, cotton, cinchona), not peasant staples (rice, wheat, millet). Imported machines failed.
  • Famine was the spur each time: Orissa 1866 → a department (closed 1879 to save money); 1876–78 famine → provincial departments (mostly data collection); agricultural chemist proposed in 1882, approved only in 1889. Voelcker (1893) praised irrigation but criticised neglect of soil chemistry and manures.
  • The 1896–1900 famines → 1901 Famine Commission: expert research is "the crying necessity of the time". Inspector-General of Agriculture (1901); Indian Agricultural Service (1906) — 42 years after forestry.
  • Pusa Institute (Bihar, 1905–06) — funded by a £30,000 gift from American philanthropist Henry Phipps; 1,300-acre farm; "full staff of European specialists" + "native assistants". Of 40 posts, only 8 Indians, mostly junior.
Albert & Gabrielle Howard — learning from Indian farmers

Voelcker had said: don't import foreign seeds; test indigenous varieties. The Howards (Gabrielle was a rare woman scientist in colonial India) studied 3 dozen Indian wheats and bred rust-resistant "Pusa" wheats. They said trying to reform Indian farming on Western lines was a "fundamental mistake"; Indian practices were "worthy of respect"; the goal was "the application of Western scientific methods to the local conditions to improve Indian agriculture on its own lines". Pusa also studied how rice fixes nitrogen (with chemist Jatindra Nath Sen).

Pusa's problems: provinces resented its advice; isolation; Europeans needed big salaries, palatial bungalows, a Europeans-only club and "the finest tennis courts in India"; loss of purpose. After the 1934 Bihar earthquake it moved to Delhi — Arnold sees this as symbolic of the fate of imperial science and its secluded institutes.

5.6 The rise of an Indian scientific community

Who were they? Where did they come from?

  • Western education in Calcutta (Hare School, Presidency College, St Xavier's) + study abroad (Gilchrist scholarships): Mahendralal Sircar (MD 1863), P. N. Bose (London, 1874), J. C. Bose (Cambridge & London), P. C. Ray (Edinburgh, 1882).
  • But also Indian roots: most were bhadralok (Brahmin, Kayastha, Vaidya) with a heritage of learning and some wealth. J. C. Bose came from Vikrampur (an old centre of Sanskrit and Buddhist learning); his Brahmo father steered him away from the ICS. Ray's father was a Persian scholar; Ray joined the Brahmo Samaj. Sircar came from a lower-ranking Sadgop family. C. V. Raman: Tamil Brahmin, mother from Sanskrit pandits; joined the Finance Department (1907), luckily posted to Calcutta.
  • Going abroad gave a sense of belonging to an international scientific community. P. N. Bose dreamed of "a niche in the modern Temple of Science". Ray: "every civilised country including Japan was adding to the world's stock of knowledge but … unhappy India was lagging behind". Coming home to racism made them more determined — a "dual dedication to science and nation".

How small the scene was (Asiatic Society, 1895)

295 members, 62% non-Indian; many ICS, IMS and army. Over half the Indian members were princes, zamindars and notables. When J. C. Bose presented his famous paper on electric waves (May 1895), only 13 members and 3 visitors turned up. The state dominated; scope for Indian scientists was small.

Wider interest across India

  • Aligarh Scientific Society (1864) — Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan; translated ~40 works into the vernacular; Akhbar Institute Gazette (1866). Bihar Scientific Society (1868). Urdu health journal Guardian of Health (Lahore, 1878–88) mixing "English and Native" ways.
  • Bengal was most active: Society for Translating European Sciences (1828); Rajendralal Mitra's science journal; between 1875 and 1896, 776 science publications in Indian languages in Bengal alone.
Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science (IACS), 1876 — Mahendralal Sircar
  • Sircar (an MD who later turned to homoeopathy) saw science as the source of the "superiority of the Western nations" and the key to India's progress and self-respect.
  • In 1869 he urged Indians to shake off "indolence and apathy", be self-reliant, and not look to the state; he wanted an institution "entirely under native management and control", with public lectures for ordinary people. It took 7 years to raise the money.
  • It proved that Indians were fit for "pure" science and research, not just artisans or "departmental drudges".
  • It turned Indian science towards physics and chemistry — sciences that did not put India and Indians "under scrutiny" (unlike colonial natural history and medicine) and that helped Indians connect with their own past.
  • Sircar: science has "a higher and nobler claim than the narrow, utilitarian, Benthamite one" — it ennobles the mind. He died disappointed (1904) at public apathy, but IACS inspired J. C. Bose, P. C. Ray and C. V. Raman, and made Calcutta the centre of national science.

Other forces that helped

  • Nationalism and Swadeshi (1905) — emphasised practical science and technical education: P. N. Bose's pamphlet on technical education (1886) and Indian Industrial Association (1891); Association for the Advancement of Scientific and Industrial Education (1904) to train Indians abroad; Dawn Society (1904) → National Council of Education → Bengal Technical Institute → later Jadavpur College of Engineering and Technology; and the University College of Science, Calcutta.
  • Universities: before 1900, little research; chairs reserved for IMS/Educational Service men. Curzon's Universities Commission and the Indian Universities Act allowed postgraduate teaching and research. From 1909, Asutosh Mukherjee (Vice-Chancellor, Calcutta) started postgraduate science; chairs in physics, chemistry, maths, botany, zoology.
  • Indian philanthropy filled the gap left by the state: Tarakanath Palit and Rash Behari Ghosh gave Rs 2.4 million for physics and chemistry chairs (the Palit chairs reserved for Indians); princely states (Baroda, Hyderabad, Mysore, Travancore) funded technical education.
  • J. N. Tata (1896) — Rs 3 million of property for an Indian university of science. Curzon narrowed it; Bangalore chosen partly because Mysore paid and the climate suited European staff. The Indian Institute of Science opened (1908–09) but struggled; ran under Europeans until C. V. Raman became its first Indian director (1933).
Indian Science Congress (1914) — from colonial to national science

First met at the Asiatic Society; President Asutosh Mukherjee praised Jones, but urged landlords and businessmen to see science as key to agriculture and "industrial regeneration", and said even "the most enlightened governments" must be reminded of science's claims on public funds. Ironically, the idea came from two Europeans (MacMahon and Simonsen), who wanted something like the British Association. One-third of the 35 papers in 1914 were by Indians. When P. C. Ray became President (1920, a strongly nationalist address) and Raman secretary (1921), the Congress became the forum of national science — as the Asiatic Society had been for colonial science.

5.7 Chemistry, physics and plant physiology: the Indian breakthrough

Why physics and chemistry?

  • Chemistry had been neglected in Company times (P. N. Bose's 1883 survey found hardly any chemistry papers). Chairs went to IMS men by seniority (O'Shaughnessy is remembered for the telegraph, not chemistry).
  • No British dominance and no state services in physics and chemistry → open to Indians (unlike medicine, where Europeans held tight control).
  • Their theoretical nature suited Indian intellectual traditions better than botany and geology — the utilitarian field sciences of "exploration and exploitation".
  • The British woke up late: German synthetic indigo ("blue terror", Curzon) ruined indigo planters; chemistry became vital for agriculture and industry. The Industrial Commission (1918) proposed an all-India Chemical Service.
P. C. Ray — chemistry as a "subversive science"
  • Paid only Rs 250/month as Assistant Professor at Presidency College despite an Edinburgh D.Sc.
  • Discovered mercurous nitrite (1895) — reprinted in Nature.
  • Founded Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works (1899) — 1,400 workers by 1926; proved Bengalis could run big business and link lab to industry.
  • Built a "School of Indian Chemistry" — 100+ papers by 1920; students placed across India; a guru–disciple (acharya, ashram-like) bond.
  • Opposed the Chemical Service (1920): the services were "an anomaly, nay, a glaring anachronism"; Indian graduates could "hold their own" — fund universities instead.
  • Chemistry was "subversive": the kind of pure science Indians were supposed to be unfit for, not a British monopoly; inspired by ancient Indian alchemy, French and German chemists, and Japan.
  • 1920: "The fate of a nation will henceforth depend more upon the achievements of its students of science than upon the skill of its generals."
J. C. Bose and C. V. Raman — physics
  • J. C. Bose — reluctantly admitted to the Educational Service; researched electric (radio) waves extending Hertz, with simple home-made equipment; practical use was later taken by others like Marconi. Praised in London (1896), Paris, Berlin — "the first Indian to win distinction through investigation in science" (Geddes).
  • Kelvin and Lister asked for a proper lab for him — the Bengal government refused. Bose: "the real laboratory is one's own mind".
  • Bose and Ray "invented national science for India as laboratory science", unlike the field sciences dominated by Europeans.
  • Later Bose studied responses in plants and metals (making plants "speak"). British physiologists mocked it as "Oriental imagination" — maybe cost him a Nobel. (Raman called it "mumbo-jumbo"!)
  • C. V. Raman built on Bose's path — cheap "sealing wax and string" physics — and won the Nobel Prize (1930) for the Raman effect.
  • Birbal Sahni on Bose (1937): Indians were "laughed at" as scientists; Bose was "the pioneer who broke this spell".

5.8 Chapter 5 in a nutshell

What "imperial science" wantedIts limits and contradictions
  • Efficient government (census, gazetteers, services)
  • Legitimacy against nationalism ("medicine alone justifies British rule")
  • Answers to famine & epidemics (agriculture, meteorology, plague research)
  • Revenue (forestry, economic products)
  • One centralised "scientific empire" led by top European experts
  • Science kept under ICS bureaucrats & London's Royal Society
  • Mostly "applied", not fundamental research
  • Racially exclusive (195 vs 18 in 1920; Medlicott vs P. N. Bose)
  • Isolated hill-station institutes, cut off from people and universities
  • Credit had to go to the Raj; Indian projects discouraged
The main point Arnold wants us to get
  1. After 1858 science became a partner of the colonial state — for governing and for legitimising British rule (peak: Curzon). But it was instrumental, bureaucratic, racially exclusive and dependent on London.
  2. At the same time, an Indian scientific community arose — through Western education and Indian intellectual heritage, nationalism, universities and Indian philanthropy.
  3. Indians deliberately chose laboratory sciences (physics, chemistry) to prove they could do original, "pure" science — turning science into national pride and a challenge to colonial racism.
  4. The two worlds — state services vs universities, imperial science vs national science — were "competing but deeply entangled". This tension shaped science in India up to independence.
