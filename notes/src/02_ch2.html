Chapter 2

Science under the Company

Book pages 19–56 · Period: roughly 1757 (Plassey) to 1858 (end of East India Company rule)
In one line: The East India Company did not "rule by science". It used science when it brought profit, military advantage or prestige, and otherwise left it to its army engineers and surgeons as a spare-time hobby. Arnold calls this a "fitful flirtation".

2.1 The Company and modern science grew up together

The English East India Company (EIC) was founded in 1600 — the same time as the Scientific Revolution. Look at how its history runs side-by-side with big moments in science:

ScienceCompany / British power in India
Royal Society of London, 1662Company already trading at Surat, Madras, Masulipatam
Buffon's Histoire naturelle, 1749Anglo-French war in the Carnatic
Linnaeus's Species plantarum (binomial naming), 1753Plassey, 1757 — British power in Bengal
Geological Society of London, 1807British control over much of India; Marathas about to fall (1818)
Lyell's Principles of Geology, 1830Company loses its trading rights, 1833
Darwin's Origin of Species, 1859Company abolished, 1858

So the Company was "well placed" to use and support science. Arnold asks: How central was science to the Company? Was it decorative/symbolic or really useful? Who did science and why? How was India shown through science?

2.2 The Company as patron: control, stinginess, self-interest

Control and gatekeeping

  • The Court of Directors in London had a "commanding position". The Company had a near monopoly over Western science in India and strictly controlled who could enter India.
  • Outsiders were rarely allowed. Exceptions: French naturalist Victor Jacquemont (died in India, 1831) and botanist Joseph Dalton Hooker (1848–50). Alexander von Humboldt asked but was never allowed.
  • Big British scientists like Banks, Lyell and Darwin never visited India. They depended on networks of Company army officers, surgeons and officials to send specimens and information. (Darwin's India informants included surgeons Malcolmson, Royle and Falconer, and museum curator Edward Blyth.)
  • Through its control of jobs in India, the Company controlled one of the richest fields of scientific employment for Britons. Even inside the Company, scientists depended on the Directors' or Governor-General's approval and money.

Stingy and erratic funding — examples

  • Roxburgh's Plants of the Coromandel Coast: Company paid £300 in the 1790s; by 1819 it cost £2,000 and the market had collapsed — a "commercial disaster" that made the Company less generous later.
  • Siwalik fossils: Falconer and Cautley shipped 40 tons of fossils to London. The Company agreed only to buy 40 copies of the catalogue at a guinea each (≈ 40 guineas) and refused the full cost; the British government gave £1,000.
  • Hooker's Flora Indica: only one volume came out (1855); full publication had to wait till after the Company ended.
  • In public, scientists praised the Company's "princely" patronage. In private, they called the Directors "cheese-monger Emperors", "scoundrely" and "knavish".

Was it all about money? Two views

Ray Desmond

The Company was not only commercial — it kept an unprofitable library and museum in London, supported scholarly books, and allowed officials to do Oriental studies.

Arnold

The Company was often attacked for misrule, and science was prestigious among Britain's ruling class. So being called "most munificent patrons of science" was politically useful. "Self-interest and scholarship often coincided" — but self-interest was frequent; scholarship got only erratic support.

  • Lord Wellesley (1804) said promoting science was a "duty" of the British government in India. But (as Marika Vicziany shows) he knew surveys could boost his reputation and deflect criticism of his wars of expansion.
  • Anton Hove (1787), a Polish botanist sent by the Board of Trade, went to Gujarat disguised as a poor physician to report on cotton and textiles — clearly commercial and political spying.

2.3 The great surveys: Mackenzie and Buchanan

  • Colin Mackenzie — survey of Mysore, 1799–1810 (after Tipu's defeat). Aim: a clear knowledge of the "extent, properties, strength and resources" of the territory, plus "objects of natural history".
  • Francis Buchanan — Madras through Mysore to Kanara (1800–1), and Bengal & Bihar (1807–14). His orders covered everything: land, soil, rivers, weather, history, plants, animals, minerals (especially those used for food, medicine, trade), forests, mines, fisheries, agriculture, people, customs, trade. He was to send "useful or rare or curious" plants to the Calcutta Botanic Garden.
What do these surveys tell us?
  • The phrase "useful or rare or curious" shows the mix of the useful and the ornamental in Company science — but it was the useful (material worth) things that were asked about most.
  • Buchanan himself loved botany and the idea of "improvement" (from Sinclair's Statistical Account of Scotland). He also had Romantic moments about waterfalls and forests.
  • Arnold: these surveys are early examples of "governmentality" (Foucault) — the state collecting information on wealth, population, resources and customs in order to govern.
  • The surveys served many purposes; the motives of individuals (Mackenzie, Buchanan) were not always the same as their paymasters'.

2.4 Mostly a spare-time, "gentlemanly" science

  • The surveys were exceptional. Usually the Company did not start scientific projects; it just tolerated its employees' private hobbies — keeping weather records, hunting fossils, noting plants and animals.
  • Official duties got in the way. In 1788 it was said that "a mere man of letters … allotting his whole time to philosophical or literary pursuits" was "a character unknown among Europeans resident in India".
  • Unlike Britain, India had few European clergymen, landlords or manufacturers to write papers or fund societies. Scientists like Roxburgh lacked time. Many died young or went home. B. H. Hodgson was a rare naturalist who stayed on.
"Far from embodying the rule of science in the service of empire, the Company's involvement in science often appeared more like a fitful flirtation… The British enjoyed the company of science; it would be excessive to suggest that they ruled by it." — Arnold, p. 25
  • Much of the science before 1858 belonged to the British tradition of "gentlemanly" (amateur) science — no special state agency, no clear agenda. The government was even uninterested in an Indian branch of the British Association for the Advancement of Science (1830s).
  • Army engineers (like Mackenzie) and surgeons (like Buchanan) gave "all-purpose expertise" — cheaper and faster than bringing specialists. Only late did specialists come: Thomas Oldham (from the Geological Survey of Ireland) came in 1851 → Geological Survey of India (1856) — and was given only "an office, a clerk and a chaprassi"!
  • The main exception: the Great Trigonometrical Survey (1818) — but it too used army men.

2.5 How colonial science was organised

Science on the march — and the Siwalik fossils

  • Early geology was written by army officers and surgeons during troop marches — Captain Franklin in Bundelkhand (1826–7); Assistant Surgeon Hardie slipping away from his regiment to look at rocks.
  • Siwalik fossils: first found by Lt. Proby Cautley and his assistants while digging the Jumna Canal (1834). Surgeon Hugh Falconer followed up in Nov 1837 — and a local raja who owned a huge mastodon tooth ("the tooth of Deo") told him where to dig: 300 fossil bones in six hours. (Indigenous knowledge!)
  • Falconer and Cautley won the Geological Society's Wollaston Medal (1837). Falconer became FRS (1845) and a big name in British geology. India could be a "springboard" for a career in London (also George Everest).
  • But Lyell, giving the medal, praised them for overcoming being "not versed in fossil osteology" and stationed on "the remote confines" — i.e. colonial science = amateurish. Falconer himself hated going back to "isolation".

Isolation → a "diffident" and "dependent" science

  • Scientists in India saw themselves as amateurs whose job was "collecting and recording with precision" — sending raw data for expert minds in Europe to theorise. Franklin: "I am not learned enough… to become an advocate for any party."
  • James Prinsep wrote 60+ articles (1825–39); could have been "the Humboldt of the east" but died at 41.
  • Oldham (1856): "We are working under the same difficulty that would affect a tradesman without his tools or a physician without medicines."

Societies, museums, journals

  • Asiatic Society of Bengal — founded January 1784 by Sir William Jones, modelled on the Royal Society. Mainly literary/linguistic, but it was the main platform for Western science in India until the Indian Science Congress (1914). Its "Physical Class" (1808, revived 1828) met erratically. Other bodies: Agricultural Society (1820), Medical and Physical Society (1823), societies in Bombay and Madras; Royal Asiatic Society in London (1823).
  • Museums: Asiatic Society museum (proposed by Nathaniel Wallich, 1814); Bombay Literary Society museum (1815); a museum of economic geology (1835, for coal) → given to the Geological Survey in 1856. Edward Blyth, full-time curator 1841–62, was so tied down he "hardly [had] been out of" Calcutta in 14 years.
  • Journals: Gleanings in Science (1829, Capt. J. D. Herbert) — filled with local items from steamboats on the Hooghly to keeping wine cool! Calcutta Journal of Natural History (1840, John M'Clelland) — "it would not pay" — folded 1847.
  • Public apathy: Geological Society of London had 700+ members; Asiatic Society usually under 250 — just 119 in 1845. Bengal's journal-reading public was under 2,000 (1829).

Science as a badge of the European elite

  • Science helped the British in India feel connected to cultured Europe and different from both Indians and lower-class Europeans (soldiers, merchants, planters) — Matthew Edney's point.
  • The Calcutta Review (1844) said "the bane of this country is ignorance" — not in "the dark recesses of native life" but "in high places" — i.e. criticism of the Company for not caring about science.

"About India, not for Indians" — the externality of science

  • Bentinck (1835) said the "great object" was to promote European literature and science among Indians. But in practice Western science was "primarily about India and Indians, and only secondarily for and by them".
  • The Asiatic Society had no Indian members until 1829 (later Ramkamal Sen became "native secretary", 1838). The Bombay Literary Society admitted Indians only after 1840 — a "departure from the original design".
But Indians were not just spectators
  • Bal Shastri Jambhekar — Brahmin from Ratnagiri; at 17 became secretary of the Bombay Educational Society (1827); translated Brougham's Treatise on … Science into Marathi; professor of mathematics at Elphinstone College (1834); ran the Anglo-Marathi journal Darpan to spread "useful" Western knowledge.
  • Calcutta's Agricultural and Horticultural Society had Indian members from the start — Dwarkanath Tagore, Radhakanta Deb — and published some work in Bengali.
  • Middle-class Indians in Calcutta and Bombay were attracted by the practical uses of Western science and wanted to know how it gave the British such power over the material world.

2.6 Science and Orientalism: from respect to rejection

  • C. A. Bayly: the "colonial information order" was built on Indian foundations; Indian knowledge systems survived "traumatised, but recognisably the same". We must recognise "Indian agency".
  • Jones's 1784 agenda for the Asiatic Society: study everything about "man and nature" in Asia — geography, history, astronomy, maths, medicine, chemistry, agriculture, music, even pottery.
  • Thomas Trautmann: this was an attempt to fit India into Western knowledge (e.g. Jones's discovery that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin are related; matching Hindu legends with Biblical chronology). Orientalism looked for connections, but ended in difference and a hierarchy of nations. India's knowledge of itself was never "adequate"; Jones even said in science "Asiatics" were "mere children".

Case 1: Astronomy

  • Very practical for the British — finding longitude for navigation and maps. Madras Observatory's Michael Topping: astronomy is "the parent and nurse of navigation".
  • Observatories built 1792–1842 at Madras, Calcutta, Lucknow, Trivandrum, Poona (by the Company or Indian allies). Europeans worked at Indian courts (J. D. Herbert at the Nawab of Awadh's Lucknow observatory, 1831).
  • At first astronomy looked like a bridge: Jai Singh's observatories were admired; Colebrooke wrote sympathetically; John Playfair (1789) said Indian astronomy was a separate, very ancient and accurate system. But Trautmann: the question was never whether Indian astronomy could teach Europe something — Indian astronomy was judged using European astronomy as the standard.
  • Later, contempt: Calcutta Review (1844) said Hindus had "retrograded", the Surya Siddhanta was only 11th–13th century, and the "Puranic system" was "a mass of absurdity" to be "demolished" by "the measuring-rod, the theodolite and the telescope". Macaulay (1835): Indian astronomy would "move laughter in girls at an English boarding school".

Case 2: Botany and plant names

  • Linnaeus made botany a project of rationalisation — one standard system (binomial names) to name plants worldwide.
  • Jones wanted Indian plants to keep their Sanskrit names and botanists to learn Sanskrit. Ignored. Roxburgh used a "motley" mix of languages; Buchanan recommended Sanskrit but wasn't consistent.
  • Buchanan complained villagers used vague names ("large", "small", "wild", "cultivated"), many names for one plant. Robert Wight got half a dozen names for the same plant within a few miles. Risky for medicinal or poisonous plants.
  • So, increasingly, "the testimony of nature was preferred to the testimony of 'natives'". Hooker — who relied on "a small army of local assistants" — still called Indian botany "a perfect chaos of new names". (Exception: Brandis in 1874 urged foresters to learn Indian names because they were more fixed.)
  • Result: Indian plant naming moved away from Sanskrit and conformed to European taxonomy — a sign of a wider trend towards conformity in colonial science.

2.7 The Great Trigonometrical Survey (GTS)

  • Before: James Rennell, first Surveyor-General (1767) — Bengal Atlas (1780), Map of Hindoostan (1783). But mapping was haphazard; distances were guesses.
  • Triangulation came from Europe (British Trigonometrical Survey 1784, Irish Ordnance Survey) — a metropolitan precedent.
  • William Lambton (infantry officer) began in 1799 using 100-foot steel chains; Madras to west coast, finished 1817. GTS founded 1818 with Lambton in charge.
  • George Everest re-measured with "Colby's compensation bars" from Britain and by 1843 measured the "great meridional arc" — 1,500 miles from Cape Comorin to Dehra Dun.
  • In technique and scale it was ahead of any survey in Europe → colonial India could generate its own techniques, not just copy. It was in effect India's first dedicated scientific service.
Why the British wanted it
  • Military: maps for war; it grew directly from Tipu's defeat (1799); later Punjab, fear of Russia.
  • Navy & trade: exact locations of ports and coasts.
  • Political: turn diverse India into "a single, mapped entity, an ordered and rationalised imperial space".
  • Scientific prestige: measure the true shape of the earth.
Historians' views
  • Cannon: it shows British policy "better than still another study of Macaulay's education minute"; "overshadowed all European geodetic operations".
  • Edney: survey as a "panopticon" to "see" and "know" India — but a "flawed geographic panopticon", an "empiricist delusion". It never covered all India (cost, terrain, illness, small staff).
  • Arnold: still remarkable that a "cash-conscious" Company supported it for decades — few projects were so consistently backed.
Indian role in the survey

Heavy reliance on Indian skill — e.g. Radhanath Sikdar (from 1831) and the "pandits" who secretly surveyed Tibet after 1860. But Indians were kept in subordinate posts. Edney: "By measuring the land… the British distinguished themselves from the Indians: they did science, the Indians did not." The survey turned "mystical, religious, Hindu space" into a "rational, scientific, imperial structure of space" (p. 43).

2.8 Geology and zoology

  • Zoology stayed amateur — not obviously "useful", no official base; driven by collectors and hunters (shikar). Jerdon's Mammals of India (1874) was written for "sportsmen and observers". Zoological Survey only in 1916.
  • Geology was slow at first because:
    • The Company and London pressure groups didn't want India to compete with Britain's copper and minerals.
    • India was supposed to stay an agricultural land.
    • Ignorance of resources, lack of experts; jungles, malaria, tigers ("tigers have no regard for science"); no deep cuttings until canals and railways; India's rocks seemed "monotonous".
  • Slowly it became exciting: kankar, laterite (Buchanan, 1800), deep alluvium under Calcutta, the Deccan lava flows, the Siwalik fossils, the Himalayas.
  • Steam changed everything. Steamships (and later railways) needed coal → Coal Committee 1836. But slow — M'Clelland (1844): "the government move slowly in matters of improvement". Oldham (1851) → GSI; 27 coalfields examined in 15 years. Geology moved from amateur hobby to a branch of the state.

2.9 Botany: profit + philanthropy

  • Botany was the most useful and the least "provincial" Company science — closest to metropolitan science. The Company traded in spices and indigo, and its servants were always sick (medicinal plants).
  • J. G. Koenig (Danish surgeon, 1778) — first naturalist employed by the Company; brought Linnaean taxonomy to India. (Earlier: Garcia d'Orta in Goa, 16th c.; Van Rheede in Malabar, 17th c.)
  • Calcutta Botanic Garden — proposed by Col. Robert Kyd (1786), approved 1787 with Banks's support. Two motives:
    • Famine protection — new food plants (sago, date palm) after Bengal's famines.
    • Profit — grow cinnamon (a Dutch monopoly), indigo, cotton, tobacco, coffee, tea, to "outstrip our rivals".
    Arnold: a "judicious blend of profit and philanthropy" — commercial self-interest in humanitarian guise.
  • Under Roxburgh (classified 2,000+ species) it became more about collecting and classifying than improving Indian farming.
  • Saharanpur garden (proposed 1817) — J. Forbes Royle (1823–31) tested cotton, tobacco; tea succeeded, cinchona failed; for "the comforts of the people" and "the wants of a great empire".
  • Other meanings: missionary William Carey saw botany as praising God ("All Thy Works Praise Thee, O Lord"); botany as art — Indian artists Vishnu Prasad and Laksman Singh painted plants.
  • Botany + medicine: of 42 botanists before 1840, 28 were surgeons. The medical service was an "all-purpose scientific agency". The Company searched for local substitutes for imported drugs. Royle studied bazaar drugs and argued Hindu medicine was older than Graeco-Arabic medicine (1837). Later he became a professor in London — another colonial career → metropolitan career.

2.10 Science in the tropics: India as "tropical"

  • Science also placed India in the natural world — finding links with Europe (universality) but also differences. The idea of "tropicality" became a central paradigm: India as a site of tropical plants, animals and diseases.
  • The idea came from the West Indies: tropics = unfit for white settlers, good for slavery and plantations.
  • India did not fit at first — it was a land of famine (Bengal 1770, ~10 million deaths), not tropical abundance. But by the 1820s–30s "tropical" was common in texts, as the earlier respect for Indian civilisation faded and India was seen as vulnerable to a cruel nature — needing European science to "redeem" it.
  • Joseph Banks saw India as a tropical estate for Britain — sugar, cotton, coffee, indigo; in 1788 he even predicted tea could grow between Bengal and Bhutan.
  • Royle (inspired by Humboldt) divided India into tropical, temperate and alpine zones — so almost any useful plant could grow somewhere in India.
  • The Himalayas became a "spiritual homecoming" for Europeans — oaks, pines, violets reminded them of home. Hooker called Darjeeling ideal for a sanatorium — a precedent for later hill-station research institutes.
Big shift by the end of Company rule

Jones and the early Orientalists saw India as an ancient (if decayed) civilisation, like Greece and Rome. Later scientists turned away from Sanskrit texts and pandits to direct observation of nature — and saw nature as a tyrant over races "bereft of modern science and technology". It became "axiomatic" that without Western science there could be no "improvement" in the tropics.

2.11 Chapter 2 in a nutshell

Why the Company engaged with scienceWhy its support was weak / erratic
  • Revenue & trade (botany, cash crops, coal)
  • Military (maps, surveys after Tipu & Marathas)
  • Knowing new territories to rule them (surveys)
  • Prestige & deflecting criticism (Wellesley)
  • Famine (botanic gardens)
  • Health of its servants (medicinal plants)
  • Science was secondary to revenue, law and order
  • Cash-conscious Directors; "commercial disaster" of Roxburgh's book
  • Fear of Indian competition (minerals)
  • Scientists busy with official duties; few, isolated, died young
  • Small, apathetic public
The main point Arnold wants us to get
  1. The Company's relationship with science was a "fitful flirtation" — opportunistic and utilitarian, not a planned "rule by science".
  2. Science was mostly done by army engineers and surgeons as gentleman amateurs, who sent raw data to Europe — a dependent, isolated science (but sometimes ahead of Europe, as in the GTS).
  3. Science also worked as identity and prestige for the European elite, and it was "about India, not for Indians".
  4. Indian knowledge and helpers were essential (raja and fossils, Sikdar, plant names, artists, informants) — but kept subordinate, and over time European science moved away from Indian knowledge towards conformity with Europe.
  5. By the end, science framed India as tropical, backward and needing Western rescue — and practical needs (coal, maps, steam) were pushing the state towards a more professional science (GSI 1851/56).
