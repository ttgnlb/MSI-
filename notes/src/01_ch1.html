Chapter 1

Introduction: Science, Colonialism and Modernity

Book pages 1–18 · This is the "theory" chapter — use it in the intro and conclusion of almost any essay.
In one line: Science in colonial India was not just "Western science arriving in India". It was shaped by three things together — India's own traditions, colonial power, and the fight over what "modern" means.

1.1 Where Arnold starts

  • Science is "a highly social activity". It cannot be "sealed off" from the values of the society where it is done. It looks different in different times and cultures — it is "contingent and culturally specific".
  • So, a history of science in India must also be a history of India — not just a story of the West throwing its science onto India.
  • Arnold says three things shaped science, technology and medicine in India over these 200 years (late 1700s to 1947):
    1. India's own traditions of science, technology and medicine, and what they left behind for the British period.
    2. Western ("colonial") science as practised in India — and its double relationship: to the colonial government in India, and to "metropolitan" science in Europe.
    3. Science as modernity — science became the main sign of being "modern", and both British (imperial ideology) and Indians (nationalism) fought over it.

The chapter discusses these three one by one. So will we.

1.2 India's scientific traditions

(a) There was no single "Indian science"

India had many traditions — oral and written, Hindu and Muslim, regional, and many borrowed from outside. Even inside the "Hindu" tradition there were many strands. For example, Vedic astronomy was linked to religion (you needed to know solstices and equinoxes to time the sacrifices correctly). Later, astronomy, trigonometry and algebra moved partly away from religion. The Surya Siddhanta (around AD 400) discussed planets, eclipses and instruments like the armillary sphere — but also cosmology and "malignant aspects of the sun and moon". So observation and belief lived side by side.

(b) The old "Orientalist" story: golden age → decline → British rescue

European Orientalist scholars (late 1700s – early 1800s) told a three-part story. Arnold calls it the Orientalist "triptych" (a picture in three panels):

  1. Ancient Hindu golden age. India equal to (or better than) Greece and Rome — gave the world zero and "Arabic" numerals. One enthusiast said: "The Asiaticks had climbed the heights of science before the Greeks had learned their alphabet."
  2. Medieval decline. Blind faith in old texts; "tradition replaced observation", "religion supplanted science". Blamed on rigid caste, and on Muslim rule after AD 1100. The Mughal break-up after 1707 = "anarchy".
  3. British rescue. British rule brought back science from "centuries of decline".
Why this story mattered

It justified British rule: "You fell, we came to save you." And it was not only the British who believed it — many Indians accepted it too. Some Indian writers still say Indian science hit its "lowest ebb" between the 12th and mid-19th centuries.

(c) The new "revisionist" view (Arnold supports this)

  • The Muslim period was creative, not destructive. Example: medicine — Ayurveda (Hindu) and Unani-tibb (Graeco-Arabic) mixed in a "creative synthesis"; vaids and hakims did not fight each other.
  • India was not isolated. It borrowed from and gave to the Middle East, Central Asia, China and South-East Asia — in agriculture, architecture, astronomy, chemistry, medicine, metallurgy, textiles, shipbuilding and arms. (Like Needham showed for China.)
  • Europe did not hit a "stagnant" India. After 1498 (Portuguese arrival), India also gained from Europe, e.g. in shipbuilding and horticulture.
  • There was a gap between artisans (craft skill) and scholars (literati) — but Europe and China had the same gap then. At Akbar's court, war and curiosity brought them together.
  • The 18th century was not "dark":
    • Raja Jai Singh's observatories (1722–39) at Jaipur, Delhi, Mathura, Ujjain and Benares.
    • Serfoji, last Maratha ruler of Tanjore — library of Indian and Western medical texts.
    • Hyderabad and Lucknow (Islamic science, Unani), Benares and Nadia (Hindu learning), Delhi — a "twilight renaissance" until 1857.
  • Printing — Jesuits brought the press to Goa in the mid-1500s, but it had little effect until the late 1700s. Arnold: this does not prove "mechanical backwardness". India adopted Western things selectively; the manuscript tradition and scribes had prestige. (Printing later exploded in the 1800s.)
  • War technology was adopted fast — Mughals, Tipu Sultan (till 1799), and Ranjit Singh's foundries at Lahore and Amritsar made heavy guns (1820s–30s).

(d) Diversity — a strength and a weakness

  • India is huge and varied, so many regional centres flourished at once (regional schools of Ayurveda and Unani; regional weaving and dyeing). If one declined, another survived.
  • But this same size and diversity made it hard for anyone — British rulers or nationalist scientists — to use science to "conquer and integrate" India into one unit.
  • Cities: the new colonial ports (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras) grew with artisans and intellectuals who came from older centres. Old centres like Benares and Delhi survived. Universities with strong science by the 1940s (Lahore, Lucknow, Allahabad, Dacca) were in cities that were already important 200 years earlier.
  • Social groups: old elites became agents of the new science — Brahmins, Vaidyas, Kayasthas became the Bengal bhadralok. India's only science Nobel before 1947 went to a Tamil Brahmin, C. V. Raman (1930). But Parsis, Indian Christians and lower castes also joined.
  • Princes were big patrons — India's first major hydro-electric scheme was in princely Mysore (1898), and the Indian Institute of Science came up in Bangalore about ten years later. Princes were often more adventurous than the British.
Arnold's point about Indian traditions

India was not a "tabula rasa" (blank slate). Because pre-colonial Indian science was open and adaptable, an interactive model explains the colonial period better than either (a) a clash between two rigid systems, or (b) an automatic Western victory. But don't overdo it — colonial rule did cause a "profound rupture" (a deep break), both material and intellectual.

1.3 "Colonial science": Basalla and his critics

Earlier histories told science in British India as simply the introduction and spread of Western ideas. Indian traditions appeared only as "superstitious opposition" or as already dead. Now historians see it as more complex and two-way. To understand this debate, start with Basalla.

George Basalla's three-phase model (1967) — "The Spread of Western Science"

PhaseWhat happens
Phase 1
Contact & collection		Europeans arrive (trade, conquest). The "non-scientific" land is just a source of data — maps, plants, animals, minerals. Main sciences: botany and zoology, then astronomy, geology, geography. Only Europe (with a "modern scientific culture") can "appreciate, evaluate and utilise" this data.
Phase 2
"Colonial science"		Local scientists (Basalla means Europeans living there) and local institutions appear. But they are dependent on Europe — trained there, follow its agenda, can't easily enter top societies or the "invisible colleges" where new ideas are debated. Basalla said dependent ≠ inferior. He applied it even to the USA and Japan.
Phase 3
"National science"		Enough local scientists; national institutions and honours; science serves national needs. The USA and Russia reached this between the two World Wars.

What Basalla got right: botany and zoology really did dominate early colonial India (and this lasted into the 20th century, even though Indians tried to push towards maths, physics and chemistry).

Criticisms of Basalla — learn these 7 points
  1. Ignores medicine and technology — which mattered more than "pure" science in 19th-century India.
  2. Treats all colonies the same. Settler colonies (Australia, North America — natives pushed aside) are very different from "colonies of exploitation" like India (few whites, strong local culture).
  3. Assumes "colonial science" = Europeans only. Indians practised it too. (And not only British: Danish botanists, German foresters, American malaria experts worked in India.)
  4. Assumes science is "value neutral, objective … universally valid" and that its spread is good and inevitable — Michael Adas calls this a "developmental teleology".
  5. Assumes colonial governments happily spread science. Often they distorted or blocked it.
  6. Eurocentric — says modern science could start only in the West; ignores India's and China's long traditions; makes non-Western people passive.
  7. Even after independence, many ex-colonies couldn't build "national science" and stayed dependent on a few scientific superpowers.

Other arguments against "diffusion"

  • The receiving culture is active. It "subverts, contaminates, and reorganises" the science that Europe brings (Raina & Habib). Caveat: before 1947, colonial political and financial control limited how far this could go.
  • Western science depended on local knowledge — "local savants, scribes, interpreters and artists, fishermen and forest-folk". Many so-called Western "discoveries" in botany, geology and the trigonometrical surveys used Indian information.

An alternative: forget "centre vs periphery"?

  • Some argue science was always cosmopolitan; the "metropolis" can move around (Roy MacLeod's "moving metropolis").
  • Colonial scientists were equal participants, not inferior; there was no real "low science" (fact-collecting in colonies) vs "high science" (theory in Europe) (Satpal Sangwan).
Arnold's reply (very important)

This view rescues colonial scientists from being looked down upon — but it ignores the "political character of science". India was a colony, not independent. Under both Company and Crown there was a clear hierarchy: Britain and its scientists at the top; India (and even British scientists working in India) dependent and subordinate. Even near the end of British rule, British authority in science stayed strong. As R. K. Kochhar says, Basalla's model "trivialises the compulsions of colonialism".

Also, science is part of a global hegemony — even Nehru's India found it hard to get "scientific salvation". And inside India, science shaped class (new industries, new professional and working-class identities, new elites).

Arnold's conclusion: keep the term "colonial science", but use it flexibly — to describe "the various technologies of power operating within and through science in a colonial setting".

1.4 Science as modernity

Science was closely tied to both British and Indian ideas of what it means to be modern.

British side
  • At first (late 1700s) the British were fairly open to Indian ideas.
  • By the early 1800s, science, technology and medicine became their "civilising mission" — proof of superiority over a "superstitious and backward" India. Science increased the sense of difference.
  • After the Industrial Revolution, steam technology (steamships, railways) became the measure of civilisation (Adas, Machines as the Measure of Men). Also: Western vs indigenous medicine; belief that only the West could master India's environment.
  • Peak: Lord Curzon (1899–1905) and "imperial science".
Indian side
  • Many Western-educated Indians embraced modernity. Gyan Prakash: "scientific reasoning became the organising metaphor" of the elite — they judged religion and society by science.
  • "Colonial modernity" reached education, health, home life, gender roles, religion and social reform.
  • But Indians differed widely in their responses.

The contradictions in British faith

  1. Indians were seen as "unready (or unfit)" to receive scientific modernity.
  2. The British wanted to deny India the competitive advantages full access to science and technology would give.
  3. A romantic anti-industrialism — India should stay "a land of princes, peasants and artisans", spared ugly industry.

The problem for Indians: modernity = the West?

  • If "modernisation" means accepting Western institutions and values (Lucian Pye's definition: advanced technology, the spirit of science, rationality, secularism, the nation-state), Indians will always be "one step behind", "second-best and imperfect copies".
  • Partha Chatterjee: Indian nationalism challenged British political rule but accepted the same idea of "modernity" on which that rule was based. (His book asks if nationalism was a "derivative discourse".)

Two ways out of this puzzle:

  1. Translation (Prakash) — Indians did not accept science exactly as the West gave it; they "translated" and renegotiated it to fit Indian idioms and needs.
  2. Multiple modernities — modernity is not one Western "patent"; it can take many forms, mixing Western science with Indian traditions.

Indian scientists did try to build their own Indian modernity, especially by selectively using (or re-inventing) Hindu ideas — with different amounts of "hybridity" in different people.

How it ended: Nehru vs Gandhi

Nehru: a worshipper at "the shrine of science"; science = both the tool and the symbol of Indian modernity. Gandhi: rejected modernity, "its alien sciences and inappropriate technologies". After 1947, under Nehru, a pragmatic compromise: tradition got only a secondary role; the state chose an internationally recognisable science and technology.

1.5 Chapter 1 in a nutshell

The main point Arnold wants us to get
  1. Science is social and political, not neutral.
  2. India had rich, plural, adaptable traditions — the Orientalist "golden age → decline → rescue" story is wrong and served colonial interests.
  3. The relationship was interactive, not one-way diffusion — but it was unequal, because of colonial power. So reject Basalla, but don't swing to the other extreme of "no centre, no periphery".
  4. Science became the core of modernity — used by the British to claim superiority and by Indians to claim equality and nationhood. This created dilemmas (copying vs translating; Nehru vs Gandhi).
Exam tip

Almost every essay can start with: "As David Arnold argues, science is 'a highly social activity' that cannot be 'sealed off' from the society in which it is practised. In colonial India, science, technology and medicine were shaped by Indian traditions, colonial power and competing ideas of modernity…" And for any centre–periphery or "colonial science" question, use the Basalla model + 7 criticisms + Arnold's reply.
