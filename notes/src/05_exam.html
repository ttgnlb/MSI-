Exam section

Essay Plans: Past Questions + Likely Questions

Each plan gives you: what the question is really asking → a thesis line (your main argument) → an intro → body points with examples → a conclusion. For a 1000-word answer, write an intro (~120 words), 6–8 body paragraphs (~100 words each), and a conclusion (~100 words).
The winning formula for any essay in this course
  1. Intro: define the key words of the question + say Arnold's big idea (science is social/political; interaction, not one-way diffusion; but under unequal colonial power).
  2. Body: one point per paragraph. Every point = claim + concrete example (name, date, number) + why it matters.
  3. Show both sides: British motives AND Indian agency; what worked AND its limits; historians who disagree (e.g. Stone vs Whitcombe, Desmond vs Arnold, MacLeod vs Arnold).
  4. Name-drop historians correctly: Basalla, Adas, Bayly, Edney, Headrick, Pacey, Kerr, Grove, Kumar, Sangwan, Prakash, Chatterjee. It shows you read.
  5. Conclusion: come back to Arnold's main message and answer the question directly in one clear sentence.

A. The 6 past test questions

Test 1 · Q1

Discuss the various characteristic features of 'modern' science.

Main source: Ch 1 (+ examples from Ch 2, 5). This question may also draw on your class lectures on the Scientific Revolution — add those points if you covered them.
Thesis

Modern science is usually described as empirical, rational, systematic and universal. But as Arnold shows, it is also a social institution — organised, professional, tied to the state, industry and power. In colonial India, these features became very visible, and "being scientific" became the main sign of being "modern".

Body points
  1. Observation, measurement and experiment over authority of texts — "the measuring-rod, the theodolite and the telescope" vs Puranic astronomy (1844); Lambton's chains and Everest's compensation bars; Bose's sensitive lab instruments.
  2. Systematic classification and a standard language — Linnaeus's binomial system (1753); Indian plant names replaced by European taxonomy; "testimony of nature" over "testimony of natives".
  3. Claims to universality and objectivity — science presented as "value neutral, objective… universally valid" (Basalla, as criticised by Adas). Arnold: actually it is "a highly social activity", culturally specific.
  4. Organised in institutions and networks — Royal Society (1662), Asiatic Society (1784), journals (Asiatic Researches, Nature), museums, botanic gardens; recognition through medals, FRS, Nobel Prize; "invisible colleges".
  5. Specialisation and professionalisation — from "gentleman amateurs" (army surgeons, engineers) to professional bodies (Oldham & GSI 1851/56; scientific services by 1914; IRFA 1911).
  6. Utility: linked to state, economy and industry — "useful knowledge", "improvement"; coal for steam, cash crops, chemistry for industry (synthetic indigo); science + technology fused in the steam age.
  7. Linked to power — knowledge to rule: surveys, census (1871), gazetteers ("scientific title deed"), "governmentality".
  8. International and cumulative — a world community adding to "the world's stock of knowledge" (P. C. Ray); Indians going abroad to join "the modern Temple of Science" (P. N. Bose).
  9. A rational, secular world-view and the idea of progress — modernity defined by "advanced technology and the spirit of science, … a rational view of life, a secular approach" (Pye); "scientific reasoning became the organising metaphor" of the Indian elite (Prakash).
  10. Laboratory vs field science — Bose, Ray and Raman made physics and chemistry (lab sciences) the face of Indian modern science.
Conclusion

Modern science is not only a method; it is an institution with authority. In India it came with colonialism, so its "modern" features also acted as markers of European superiority — which Indians then claimed for themselves.

Test 1 · Q2

Discuss the nature of the relationship between science and the East India Company in its early years in India.

Main source: Ch 2
Thesis

The relationship was erratic, opportunistic and utilitarian — a "fitful flirtation" rather than "rule by science". The Company supported science when it served profit, war or prestige; otherwise science depended on individual army engineers and surgeons working in their spare time.

Intro idea

The EIC (1600) was "as old as modern science itself" — its rise in India ran parallel to Linnaeus (1753), Plassey (1757), Lyell and Darwin. It was well placed to use science, but how central was science to it?

Body points
  1. Gatekeeper and monopolist — Court of Directors controlled entry (Humboldt refused; Hooker an exception), appointments and funding; London scientists (Banks, Lyell, Darwin) depended on Company servants.
  2. Stingy, erratic patron — Roxburgh's book a "commercial disaster" (£300 → £2,000); only 40 guineas for the Siwalik catalogue vs £1,000 from government; "cheese-monger Emperors".
  3. Self-interest + prestige — Desmond says not purely commercial (library, museum); Arnold: being "munificent patrons of science" was politically useful; Wellesley used surveys to boost his image (Vicziany).
  4. Utilitarian surveys = "governmentality" — Mackenzie (Mysore 1799–1810), Buchanan (1800–1, 1807–14): resources, revenue, people; plants "useful or rare or curious". Hove's cotton spying (1787).
  5. Mostly "gentlemanly" spare-time science — officers busy with duties; few societies; Asiatic Society (1784) mainly literary, only 119 members in 1845; journals folded ("it would not pay").
  6. Army engineers and surgeons as all-purpose experts — 28 of 42 botanists were surgeons; professionals only late (Oldham 1851: "an office, a clerk and a chaprassi").
  7. Where the Company did commit: utility — Great Trigonometrical Survey (1818) for war and control (India's first dedicated scientific service); Calcutta Botanic Garden (1787) for profit and famine; Coal Committee (1836) for steam.
  8. Dependent and isolated science — Lyell's condescension; Oldham: "a tradesman without his tools"; data sent to Europe for theory.
  9. Science "about India, not for Indians" — no Indian members of the Asiatic Society till 1829; but Indians still engaged (Bal Shastri Jambhekar; Dwarkanath Tagore).
  10. From Orientalist respect to dismissal — astronomy and plant names; India framed as "tropical" and in need of rescue.
Conclusion

"The British enjoyed the company of science; it would be excessive to suggest that they ruled by it" (p. 25). But by the 1830s–50s, practical needs (maps, coal, steam) were pushing the Company towards professional state science — the base for the Crown's "imperial science".

Test 2 · Q1

Science and technology were loaded with diverse functions and meanings in colonial India. Discuss.

Main sources: all four chapters — this is the best "show everything you know" question.
Thesis

In colonial India, science and technology were never just about knowing nature. They worked as a tool (profit, war, control), a symbol (superiority, civilising mission), a source of legitimacy, a marker of identity — and later as a weapon of Indian nationalism and a target of critique. Their meaning depended on who used them and why.

Body points (pick 7–8)
  1. Economic — Kyd's garden to "outstrip our rivals" (cinnamon, indigo, tea); coal; forestry revenue; railways to "intermesh" Britain and India's economies.
  2. Military/strategic — surveys after Tipu (1799); Grand Trunk Road; Dalhousie's 1853 railway minute; telegraph in 1857; Arms Act (1878) and closing of Ajmer mines.
  3. Knowledge for control — Buchanan's surveys, GTS as "panopticon" (Edney), census 1871, Imperial Gazetteer as "scientific title deed".
  4. Civilising mission and superiority — Hardinge: steam "the greatest instrument of civilisation"; Dalhousie: "great engines of social improvement"; Macaulay mocking Indian astronomy; Edney: "they did science, the Indians did not".
  5. Legitimacy after 1857 — technology as safer than social reform; Queen's Proclamation; Curzon: medicine "alone" justifies British rule; Begum of Awadh's sarcastic reply.
  6. Identity and prestige of Europeans — science as a badge of the elite (Edney); medals and FRS as career springboards (Falconer); Company as "munificent patron".
  7. Humanitarian face — famine: botanic gardens, "protective" railways (1880), canals, agricultural research (Pusa).
  8. Religious, aesthetic and emotional — Carey's botany praising God; Himalayas as a "spiritual homecoming"; Cotton's belief that irrigation would spread Christianity.
  9. Racial meaning — tropicality; hill-station labs; Lansdowne on "races bred in a tropical climate"; Medlicott on Bengalis.
  10. For Indians: modernity, reform, nationhood — Prakash's "organising metaphor"; Sircar's IACS (science = source of Western superiority); Ray: "the fate of a nation…"; Bose "broke this spell"; Swadeshi industry.
  11. Critique — Keshab Sen ("materialistic"); Birdwood/Havell's romantic anti-industrialism; Gandhi: machinery "a great sin", railways "propagate evil"; charkha as counter-symbol.
  12. Everyday meanings — railways for pilgrims and travel (Pather Panchali); telegrams for business and politics; rumours and resistance to plague measures.
Conclusion

Science and technology were "multi-meaning": the same railway was a tool of empire, a symbol of civilisation, a carrier of pilgrims and a sign of slavery for Gandhi. This proves Arnold's point that science is social and political, never neutral.

Test 2 · Q2

Analyse the multiple ways in which the 'centre' and 'periphery' influenced each other (with regard to science and technology) during East India Company rule.

Main sources: Ch 1 (Basalla & critics) + Ch 2 (examples). Stay within c. 1757–1858.
Define first

Centre = the metropole: Britain/London — Court of Directors, Royal Society, Kew, British scientists, British industry. Periphery = the colony: India — Company servants, Indian informants and workers, Calcutta/Bombay/Madras institutions.

Thesis

Basalla's diffusion model sees a one-way flow from centre to periphery. Arnold shows influence ran both ways — India supplied data, discoveries, techniques and careers that changed metropolitan science — but always inside an unequal, political hierarchy where the centre kept authority.

Centre → periphery
  1. Control and patronage — Court of Directors controlled access, money, jobs; refusal to Humboldt.
  2. Institutional models — Royal Society → Asiatic Society (1784); BAAS as the model (M'Clelland's failed attempt).
  3. Methods and instruments — Linnaean taxonomy (Koenig 1778); triangulation from the British Trigonometrical Survey and Irish Ordnance Survey; Colby's bars; Oldham from Ireland.
  4. Standards and judgement — Indian astronomy judged by European standards (Trautmann); Indian plant names replaced; Lyell's condescension ("remote confines").
  5. Economic demands — Banks's vision of India as a tropical estate; Board of Trade's spy Hove; London lobby against Indian copper/mineral competition.
Periphery → centre
  1. Raw data and specimens — 40 tons of Siwalik fossils; plants to Kew; Darwin's India informants (Falconer, Royle, Blyth).
  2. New knowledge that changed metropolitan science — Siwalik fossils for debates on extinction and climate; Deccan traps, laterite; Royle's tropical–temperate–alpine zones; the idea of "tropicality".
  3. Innovation ahead of Europe — GTS "overshadowed all European geodetic operations" (Cannon), measured the earth's shape; Ganges Canal became a model for the USA and Australia; Bombay-built ships for the Royal Navy.
  4. Colonial careers became metropolitan careers — Falconer (Wollaston Medal 1837, FRS 1845), Royle (King's College), Everest, Hooker.
  5. Indian knowledge entered Western science — the raja and the "tooth of Deo"; Royle on the antiquity of Hindu medicine; Jones on Sanskrit–Greek–Latin; Sikdar and the pandits; Indian artists' plant drawings.
  6. Conservation ideas — Company surgeons' warnings about deforestation and climate (Grove argues the colonies were at the "cutting edge").
The limits (show Arnold's balance)
  • Isolation and dependence: Oldham's "tradesman without his tools"; "collecting and recording" for Europe to theorise; Falconer hating his return; Blyth stuck in Calcutta.
  • Sangwan/MacLeod argue colonial scientists were equal and the metropolis "moves" — Arnold replies this ignores the "political character of science": a clear hierarchy put Britain on top. Indians stayed subordinate (Edney: "they did science, the Indians did not").
  • Over time, conformity with the centre grew (plant names).
Conclusion

Centre and periphery shaped each other — it was an interaction, not a diffusion — but the interaction was unequal. As Arnold says, Basalla "trivialises the compulsions of colonialism"; yet ignoring the centre–periphery hierarchy altogether would be just as wrong.

Test 3 · Q1

Highlight and discuss some of the salient features that are common to the introduction of almost all technologies during the era of British rule in India.

Main source: Ch 4 (+ Ch 2 for surveys, Ch 5 for institutes). Use railways, telegraph, irrigation, steamships, textiles, mining as your examples.
Thesis

The introduction of technology was not a simple "transfer" but a "dialogue" on colonial terms. Almost every technology shows the same pattern: driven by state needs, supplied from Britain, restricted when it threatened British interests, reshaped by Indian conditions and labour, coexisting with older technologies, loaded with ideology, and received in mixed ways by Indians.

Body points (common features)
  1. State-driven for military, administrative and economic needs — roads (GTR), railways (Dalhousie 1853: news and troops), telegraph (Rangoon 1852, 1857), Ganges steamers (mail), Calcutta Mint (1831).
  2. Imported from Britain → dependence; British firms profit — 90% of textile machinery from Lancashire; prefabricated steamships; rails, engines, even drivers; 14,000 locomotives sold vs ~700 made in India; workshops only for repair.
  3. Denial as important as diffusion — Indian shipbuilding blocked (Sangwan); locomotive making thwarted; Arms Act 1878; Ajmer mines closed 1857; no tariffs for handlooms.
  4. Adapted to Indian conditions ("in situ" innovation) — O'Shaughnessy's thick insulated cables; Indian well-sinking for bridge piers; Ganges Canal "shaped bit by bit to the circumstances of the country"; took decades (railway building "routinised" only by the 1890s); some failures (Indus steamers, steam ploughs).
  5. Built on cheap Indian labour, tools and methods — Kerr: labour "worth many a machine"; basket labour; Bhor Ghat 42,000 workers; earth-and-rubble anicuts.
  6. Indians kept in subordinate roles — exceptions prove the rule: Shib Chandra Nandy (before posts were "Europeanised"), Cursetjee, Sikdar.
  7. Old and new side by side — handlooms (1/3 of output in the 1930s), wells and tanks (~60% of irrigation in 1901), bullock-carts feeding railways.
  8. Famine as a driving force — "protective" railways (1880 Commission: 20,000 miles); canals after famines (Godavari after 1833; Ganges Canal after 1837–38); roads by famine labour.
  9. Heavy ideology — technology as proof of civilisation and superiority; monuments "like Rome" (MacGeorge, Deakin); used for legitimacy after 1857.
  10. Pushed by individuals and army engineers — Bentinck, Dalhousie ("technophile"), Curzon; Cotton, Cautley; "technocratic paternalism".
  11. Unintended costs — de-industrialisation of crafts; waterlogging, reh, malaria (Whitcombe vs Stone); disease spread by railways.
  12. Mixed Indian responses — fast adoption (railway "naturalised in the native mind", 1858; pilgrims; telegrams), selective resistance (1857 strategic attacks; plague measures), critique (Keshab Sen, Gandhi) and Swadeshi counter-industry.
Conclusion

These shared features show Arnold's argument: colonial technology was at once a tool of empire, a dialogue with India, and an act of denial — and its meaning stayed contested right up to Gandhi's charkha.

Test 3 · Q2

Discuss the place and roles of Indians and pre-existing Indian methods and materials during the introduction of technologies into India by the British colonial power.

Main source: Ch 4 (+ Ch 2 survey/fossils/botany, Ch 5 Howards/forestry)
Thesis

Against the "pure transfer" story, Indians and Indian methods were central — as labour, skilled artisans, intermediaries, technical assistants, entrepreneurs, sources of materials and techniques, and as users and critics. But colonial power kept them subordinate, often looked down on their methods, and sometimes suppressed Indian technology.

Body points
  1. Indian materials — teak for ships (even steamer hulls like the Hugh Lindsay); kankar as "Indian macadam" on the Grand Trunk Road; Raniganj coal; Salem ore for Porto Novo steel; coir and lime in shipbuilding.
  2. Indian techniques adopted by the British — rabbeting (no caulking); the well-sinking method for bridge piers; Grand Anicut's earth-and-rubble design copied by Cotton, who credited the "old native engineers"; restoring Mughal canals (Shah Jahan's Delhi canal).
  3. Indian labour as the backbone — 42,000 on the Bhor Ghat; Santal and Bauri families in coal mines; basket labour on the Ganges Canal; customary tools beat wheelbarrows (Kerr); quick learning of new skills (riveting).
  4. Skilled artisans and intermediaries — Parsi Master Builder Lowji Nusserwanji Wadia (159 ships at Bombay 1736–1821); Wudder well-diggers; jobbers in mills; Julaha weavers.
  5. Technical assistants and pioneers — Shib Chandra Nandy (telegraph lines), Radhanath Sikdar and the pandits (survey), Ardaseer Cursetjee (engineer, FRS), Lalla Tantimul (horse dak, 1849).
  6. Indian entrepreneurs and capital — Dwarkanath Tagore (Raniganj, 1836); Parsis → Tata; P. N. Bose finding Mayurbhanj iron ore for Tata; Swadeshi ventures; P. C. Ray's Bengal Chemicals.
  7. Pre-existing systems that continued and adapted — handlooms with fly-shuttle, jacquard and electricity; wells and tanks; bullock-carts and country boats; Birdwood's praise of the "Mahratta plough"; the Howards' respect for Indian farming "on its own lines".
  8. Indians as users who gave new meanings — railways for pilgrimage and self-discovery; telegrams for business and nationalism.
  9. Indian resistance and critique that shaped outcomes — Banjaras and boatmen refusing to carry coal (Narmada); strategic attacks in 1857; resistance to plague measures; Gandhi's charkha.
But — the limits
  • Indians strictly in subordinate posts; posts became "Europeanised" (telegraph).
  • Indian methods belittled — "crude" tools (Buchanan), "art but no science" (Ball), crafts sent to the ethnographic museum or linked to "criminal tribes".
  • Indian capacity suppressed — shipbuilding, iron, metals, locomotives ("denial as important as diffusion").
Conclusion

Colonial technology "nestled" with Indian technology in a dialogue (Pacey). Indian agency was indispensable but constrained — exactly Arnold's picture of interaction under unequal power.

B. Likely new questions (shorter plans)

Likely Q7 · Ch 1

Critically assess George Basalla's model of the spread of Western science with reference to colonial India.

  • Explain the 3 phases (data source → dependent colonial science → national science).
  • What fits India: botany and zoology dominated (Kyd, Roxburgh, Buchanan); colonial scientists dependent on London (Royal Society; Lyell; Board of Scientific Advice reporting to London); Indian Science Congress (1914), IISc, Raman's Nobel look like a move toward "national science".
  • Critique (7 points) — ignores medicine/technology; one model for all colonies; colonial science = Europeans only; science as neutral and its spread inevitable (Adas); colonial states blocked science ("denial", racism); Eurocentric/passive recipients (India's own traditions — Jai Singh, Ayurveda–Unani, wootz); ex-colonies stay dependent.
  • Evidence of interaction: local knowledge (raja & fossils, Sikdar, Wudders, well-sinking piers); recipient cultures "subvert, contaminate, reorganise" (Raina & Habib); India sometimes ahead (GTS).
  • Arnold's middle way: reject diffusion but keep "colonial science" as "technologies of power operating within and through science in a colonial setting".
Likely Q8 · Ch 5

What was "imperial science"? Discuss its aims, features and limitations.

  • Definition: post-1858, esp. under Curzon — India-wide, state-run, centrally directed science serving India and the empire, staffed by European experts (services, institutes).
  • Aims: efficient government; legitimacy against nationalism (Curzon on medicine); famine and epidemics; revenue; India as one "scientific empire".
  • Features: census, gazetteers; Board of Scientific Advice (1903); institutes (Muktesar 1898, Pusa 1905, Dehra Dun 1906, Kasauli 1906); IRFA 1911; nearly a dozen all-India services by 1914; "service ethos".
  • Limitations: controlled by ICS ("Secretariat interference"); dependent on the Royal Society; mostly "applied"; racist (195 vs 18; Medlicott); isolated hill stations (Lansdowne; frog's heart); vaccine production swamped research; Pusa's isolation and move to Delhi; credit monopolised by the Raj (Tata's institute narrowed).
  • Conclusion: it raised science's status but kept it subordinate, exclusive and dependent — provoking Indian "national science".
Likely Q9 · Ch 5

Trace the emergence of an Indian scientific community between the 1870s and 1920s. What factors helped and hindered it?

  • Helped: Western education (Hare, Presidency, St Xavier's) + study abroad (Gilchrist scholarships); bhadralok heritage of learning; IACS (1876) and Sircar's vision; vernacular science societies (Aligarh 1864, Bihar 1868, Bengal's 776 publications); Swadeshi and technical education (Dawn Society, Jadavpur); Universities Act and Asutosh Mukherjee (1909); Indian philanthropy (Palit, Ghosh, Tata, princes); Indian Science Congress (1914 → Ray 1920, Raman 1921).
  • Hindered: racism in services (Medlicott vs P. N. Bose; pay gaps for J. C. Bose, Ray); small, state-dominated scene (13 people at Bose's 1895 talk); state kept research in services, not universities; Curzon's coolness to Indian projects; Western scepticism (Bose's plant work as "Oriental imagination").
  • Choices: physics and chemistry (no British monopoly; theoretical; suited Indian legacies) → laboratory science as "national science"; Ray's "subversive science"; Bose "broke this spell"; Raman's Nobel (1930).
Likely Q10 · Ch 1, 4, 5

How did Indians respond to Western science and technology? / Science and modernity in colonial India.

  • Enthusiasm: Western-educated elite — science as "organising metaphor" (Prakash); Jambhekar's Marathi translations; Calcutta and Bombay welcomed railways; Keshab Sen praised steam.
  • Science for nationhood: Sircar (IACS), Ray ("fate of a nation"), Bose, Raman, Nehru's "shrine of science"; Swadeshi industries; Tata.
  • Translation / hybridity: science renegotiated in Indian idioms; selective use of Hindu ideas; Ray drawing on ancient Indian alchemy and metallurgy; Bose's plant responses; Urdu journals mixing "English and Native" medicine.
  • Critique: Chatterjee's dilemma (nationalism accepting colonial premises of modernity); Keshab Sen's warning about materialism; Gandhi's Hind Swaraj and the charkha.
  • Popular responses: pilgrims on trains; resistance to plague measures and survey parties; rumours — but British exaggerated "hostility" (1857 destruction was strategic).
Likely Q11 · Ch 2, 4, 5 (great cross-chapter question)

Discuss the role of famine in shaping science and technology in colonial India.

  • Company period: Bengal famine 1770 (~10 million) → Kyd's botanic garden (1787) for famine food plants; Roxburgh at Samalkot; India seen as "tropical" and vulnerable; surgeons link deforestation to drought (Grove).
  • Steam age: canals after famines (Godavari after 1833; Ganges Canal after 1837–38); 1880 Famine Commission → 20,000 miles of "protective" railways; famine labour building roads; railways as laissez-faire-friendly famine policy.
  • Crown: Orissa famine (1866) → meteorology and an agriculture department; Pogson's sunspot–famine claim → Kodaikanal (1895); 1896–1900 famines → 1901 Famine Commission → Inspector-General of Agriculture, Pusa, Agricultural Service (1906).
  • Limits: technology favoured over science; canals' environmental costs; Gandhi: railways "encouraged famine".
  • Conclusion: famine pushed science and technology towards agrarian rather than industrial goals — a very "Indian" pattern.
Likely Q12 · Ch 1, 2, 4, 5

Discuss the relationship between Western science and indigenous knowledge in colonial India.

  • Pre-colonial plurality — not a blank slate (Ch 1); Orientalist "triptych" vs revisionists.
  • Early dependence and respect — Bayly's "information order" on Indian foundations; Jones's Sanskrit names; Royle on Hindu medicine; Jai Singh's observatories; raja and fossils; local guides for Hooker.
  • Growing dismissal — Trautmann (European standard); Macaulay; plant names replaced; "testimony of nature over natives"; crafts as "crude" and to the museum; Edney's "they did science, the Indians did not".
  • Practical borrowing continued — well-sinking, anicuts, rabbeting, Wudders; foresters learning from shifting cultivators; tribals' knowledge of species and drugs.
  • Late re-appreciation — Howards and Voelcker (indigenous wheat); Birdwood/Havell on crafts; Brandis on Indian plant names.
  • Indian re-use of tradition — Ray drawing on ancient alchemy; Sircar's physics/chemistry connecting Indians to their past; Gandhi's charkha.
Likely Q13 · Ch 5

Examine the relationship between science and the colonial state after 1858.

  • Alliance: science for legitimacy and governance (census, gazetteers, services, institutes, Curzon).
  • Subordination: "science existed to serve the empire"; ICS control; Watt's "Secretariat interference"; Ross resigns; Day's fisheries abolished; Voelcker on generalist directors.
  • Dependence on London: Royal Society Advisory Committee; Board of Scientific Advice reports to London; only applied research (MacLeod vs Arnold).
  • Exclusion: racial "apartheid" (Kumar); Table 5.1; hill stations.
  • State vs universities: research kept in state institutes; Ray attacks services as "a glaring anachronism"; Indian philanthropy funds universities.
  • Conclusion: a "competing but deeply entangled" relationship — the state made science big but not free.
Likely Q14 · Ch 4

"Modern technology was the British Raj's greatest monument." Discuss with reference to railways and irrigation.

  • British view: Hardinge, Dalhousie, MacGeorge, Deakin ("would fully justify their supremacy"); Ganges Canal unsurpassed; 40,000 miles of track; technology over social reform after 1857.
  • Real gains: faster transport and news; famine relief; irrigated 1 million acres; Stone's optimistic view; railway workshops as "schools of skills".
  • But: "a British show" — imports, 14,000 vs 700 locomotives; built for war and trade; dependent on Indian labour and methods; environmental costs (Whitcombe); old wells (60%) and carts still essential; Gandhi's critique; the Begum of Awadh's jibe.
  • Conclusion: a monument — but to colonial priorities, not to balanced Indian development.
Likely Q15 · Ch 4

Discuss the debate on industrialisation and technology in late colonial India (Birdwood, Havell, Swadeshi, Gandhi).

  • Western romantic critique: Ruskin, Morris → Birdwood (crafts as art; steam-plough worshipped as a lingam); Havell (revive handlooms with the fly-shuttle; avoid European industrial evils).
  • Indian suspicion: anti-industrialism seen as keeping India "second-best" and dependent; Swadeshi (1905) mills, match factories, steamship lines — many failed.
  • Indian modernisers: P. N. Bose on technical education; Ray's Bengal Chemicals; Tata; Science Congress urging industrial regeneration.
  • Gandhi: Hind Swaraj (1909) — machinery "a great sin", railways "propagate evil", charkha and khadi as swaraj; huge influence after 1918–20.
  • Link to Ch 1: Nehru vs Gandhi; post-1947 compromise favouring modern science.
Likely Q16 · Ch 5

Medicine as "imperial science": discuss with reference to plague and medical research.

  • Neglect before 1890s — Ross, Hart ("pestilential fellow with a new bug"), "we are simply doctors".
  • Outsiders lead — Hankin, Haffkine; Ross's Nobel (1902).
  • Plague 1896 — international pressure (Venice 1897); Epidemic Diseases Act; resistance; Malkowal (1902) and Haffkine's dismissal; political control of medicine.
  • Research system — Kasauli, King Institute, Haffkine Institute, IRFA (1911), IJMR, Calcutta School of Tropical Medicine.
  • Ideology — Curzon: medicine "alone" justifies British rule; but research white-dominated, in hill stations, swamped by vaccine work.
